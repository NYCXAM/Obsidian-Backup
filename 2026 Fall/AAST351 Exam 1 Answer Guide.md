## Week 1 — Media and Cultural Theory

### 1. What role does Orientalism play in Western culture?

**Orientalism helps establish Western identity and authority by constructing an inferior Eastern “Other.”** Representations of the East as irrational, exotic, backward, or dangerous make the West appear rational, modern, and entitled to rule. These ideas help legitimate colonial domination. [Said, *Orientalism*, introduction](https://sites.evergreen.edu/politicalshakespeares/wp-content/uploads/sites/33/2014/12/Said_full.pdf).

### 2. What does Orientalism mean, and why is it used?

Said gives **three connected meanings**: an academic field studying the Orient; a way of thinking that divides East from West; and an institutional discourse through which Western powers describe, classify, administer, and dominate the East. Its power comes from presenting interested representations as authoritative knowledge. [Said, introduction](https://sites.evergreen.edu/politicalshakespeares/wp-content/uploads/sites/33/2014/12/Said_full.pdf).

### 3. How does Orientalism help analyze Asian American representation?

**Ask how a portrayal produces racial difference and whose authority it supports.** For example, Fu Manchu makes Asian intelligence appear sinister; the submissive Asian woman makes domination appear desirable. An Orientalist reading asks who speaks, who is denied an individual perspective, and whether Asian Americans are treated as permanent outsiders. This applies Said's framework to the course's media examples. [Said](https://sites.evergreen.edu/politicalshakespeares/wp-content/uploads/sites/33/2014/12/Said_full.pdf).

## Week 2 — Culture, Ideology, Hegemony, and Representation

### 4. Why are cultural texts not neutral, according to Kellner and Durham?

**Cultural texts are produced within social relations and carry values, interests, and assumptions.** Choices about protagonists, conflicts, casting, and endings organize what appears normal or desirable. A text may reinforce dominant institutions or challenge them; even entertainment has political effects. Bias does not require a deliberate conspiracy by its creators. [Kellner and Durham, “Adventures in Media and Cultural Studies”](https://pages.gseis.ucla.edu/faculty/kellner/essays/keyworks.pdf).

### 5. What is hegemony's role in society?

**Hegemony secures consent to a dominant group's leadership.** Cultural institutions make a particular social order seem legitimate and natural. Consent helps stabilize rule alongside coercive institutions such as police and the military. Hegemony can be challenged and must be maintained. [Kellner's discussion of Gramsci](https://pages.gseis.ucla.edu/faculty/kellner/essays/culturalmarxism.pdf).

### 6. How do past ruling-class ideologies shape classes and ideas today?

**Inherited institutions and ideas shape the present, but they change through conflict and changing material conditions.** Past distributions of wealth, education, and authority influence whose ideas circulate. A possible application: a story that explains wealth entirely through individual effort can preserve an older justification of hierarchy in contemporary language. Historical continuity does not mean every class or ideology remains identical. [Marx and Engels, *The German Ideology*](https://www.marxists.org/archive/marx/works/1845/german-ideology/ch01b.htm).

### 7. Does ideology operate differently in mass film and fine art?

**Neither budget nor audience size alone determines ideological power.** Applying Gramsci: commercial film may circulate assumptions widely through financing, marketing, and familiar genres. Fine art may reproduce hierarchy through patrons, museums, training, and definitions of legitimate taste. These institutions have different audiences and pressures, so their effects are not identical. Both can also support opposition. Analyze ownership, access, circulation, and reception rather than declaring either space automatically more ideological. [Gramsci, “Cultural Themes: Ideological Material”](https://web.mit.edu/uricchio/Public/Documents/Gramsci.pdf).

### 8. How does Gramsci change how we think about media?

**Media become institutions involved in organizing consent and struggle.** We ask how their routines and resources make some worldviews familiar, and how alternative institutions might support different ones. Analysis therefore includes production and institutional power, not only whether a character is offensive. [Gramsci, selections](https://web.mit.edu/uricchio/Public/Documents/Gramsci.pdf).

### 9. What are Hall's three decoding positions?

- **Dominant-hegemonic:** Accept the message within its preferred ideological framework.
- **Negotiated:** Accept the general framework but modify it for particular circumstances.
- **Oppositional:** Understand the preferred meaning but interpret it through a competing framework.

These describe positions toward a message, not permanent personality types. [Hall, “Encoding/Decoding”](https://spkb.blot.im/_readings/EncodingDecoding_HALL_1980.pdf).

### 10. What is wrong with the traditional communication model?

**Its linear sender–message–receiver model overlooks the distinct social conditions of production and reception.** It treats communication too much like transparent transmission. Encoding a meaning does not guarantee that audiences decode that same meaning. [Hall](https://spkb.blot.im/_readings/EncodingDecoding_HALL_1980.pdf).

### 11. What are preferred meanings?

**They are the interpretations a text privileges through its codes and dominant cultural assumptions.** They guide interpretation without guaranteeing it. Audiences may recognize the preferred meaning and still negotiate or oppose it. [Hall](https://spkb.blot.im/_readings/EncodingDecoding_HALL_1980.pdf).

### 12. How do different audience interpretations challenge traditional communication theory?

**Meaning is actively produced in decoding, rather than delivered intact to a passive audience.** Social position and cultural knowledge shape readings. However, interpretation is not unlimited: textual codes and unequal power still constrain it. [Hall](https://spkb.blot.im/_readings/EncodingDecoding_HALL_1980.pdf).

### 13. How does Hall approach the critique of representation?

**Examine how signs and shared codes produce meaning, rather than treating images as transparent mirrors.** Ask how categories are constructed, which meanings become authoritative, and what alternatives are excluded. This goes beyond checking accuracy to investigating the relationship between representation and power. **Reading ambiguity:** the study guide does not identify a separate Hall representation text; this answer uses his constructionist account in “The Work of Representation.” [Hall, chapter](https://www.ces.uc.pt/ficheiros2/files/HallStuart_The%20Work%20of%20Representation(1).pdf).

### 14. How does the ruling class maintain cultural hegemony?

**By organizing consent through civil society and presenting its leadership as serving general interests.** Schools, religion, media, and other institutions can make dominant values everyday common sense. Force remains available, but cultural hegemony specifically concerns consent and leadership. [Kellner on Gramsci](https://pages.gseis.ucla.edu/faculty/kellner/essays/culturalmarxism.pdf).

### 15. Why can inequality persist alongside democracy and equality?

**Formal equality can coexist with ideas that justify unequal outcomes.** Applying Gramsci, schools, news, entertainment, and everyday language may normalize existing authority. For example, a success story that attributes every outcome to effort can make structural disadvantage disappear from view. People may consent to unequal arrangements because they appear fair or inevitable. Such consent is contested, rather than complete. [Gramsci, ideological institutions](https://web.mit.edu/uricchio/Public/Documents/Gramsci.pdf).

## Week 3 — Asian American Cinema, Yellowface, and Orientalism

### 16. What does “Asian” mean in England in Feng's article?

**Primarily Pakistani and/or Indian: what U.S. usage often calls South Asian.** Feng uses this contrast to show that racial labels depend on social context, rather than having a universal meaning. This describes his 1995 comparison, not every contemporary British usage. [Feng, article transcription](https://www.studocu.com/en-us/document/northern-arizona-university/asian-americans-in-film/in-search-of-asian-american-cinema/44204189).

### 17. How does Feng challenge Hollywood's construction of Asians as racial objects?

**He centers Asian American perspectives and the power to make meaning.** Hollywood's stock figures make Asians recognizable objects within dominant fantasies; Asian American media can instead define communities and explore varied experiences. This comparison applies Feng's emphasis on perspective to Benshoff and Griffin's account of stereotyped casting. [Feng](https://www.studocu.com/en-us/document/northern-arizona-university/asian-americans-in-film/in-search-of-asian-american-cinema/44204189); [Benshoff and Griffin, Chapter 6 transcription](https://studylib.net/doc/26339404/benshoff--harry-m.-griffin--sean---america-on-film--repre...).

### 18. How does Feng define Asian American cinema? Do you agree?

**He questions a single fixed definition**, examining authorship, subject matter, markets, production practices, and the historical movement. He discusses Judi Nihei's narrower combination of Asian American stories and filmmakers, but ends by emphasizing perspectives without resolving all boundaries. A possible response: this openness is useful because a rigid ethnic-content test excludes filmmakers exploring other subjects. [Feng](https://www.studocu.com/en-us/document/northern-arizona-university/asian-americans-in-film/in-search-of-asian-american-cinema/44204189).

### 19. What is Feng's critique of representation?

**Provisional interpretation:** Question who defines Asian American experience and which works the category includes. A narrow definition can impose one supposedly authentic identity. The accessible article does not provide a distinct formal definition called “the critique of representation”; check whether lecture or another Feng excerpt supplies that wording. [Feng](https://www.studocu.com/en-us/document/northern-arizona-university/asian-americans-in-film/in-search-of-asian-american-cinema/44204189).

### 20. How did yellowface limit Asian American actors?

**White performers received major Asian roles while Asian actors were confined to smaller or stereotyped parts.** This restricted earnings, visibility, experience, and opportunities for stardom. Exclusion then became its own excuse: studios claimed too few established Asian stars existed. Benshoff and Griffin discuss this pattern and actors' frustration with typecasting. [Benshoff and Griffin](https://studylib.net/doc/26339404/benshoff--harry-m.-griffin--sean---america-on-film--repre...); [Ito](https://brightlightsfilm.com/certain-slant-brief-history-hollywood-yellowface/).

### 21. Why did Hollywood use yellowface and Orientalist stereotypes?

**Racist assumptions and industry incentives reinforced each other.** Studios protected white stars and claimed audiences required them; familiar caricatures made characters instantly legible; Orientalism supplied stories of Asian danger or submissiveness. These choices reveal that representation concerns control over employment, narrative, and authority as well as accuracy. This is a synthesis of the readings. [Ito](https://brightlightsfilm.com/certain-slant-brief-history-hollywood-yellowface/); [Said](https://sites.evergreen.edu/politicalshakespeares/wp-content/uploads/sites/33/2014/12/Said_full.pdf).

### 22. What justifications does Robert B. Ito identify?

**Hollywood claimed that audiences preferred white stars, qualified Asian actors were unavailable, or white performers made more convincing Asian characters.** Ito exposes the circularity: denying actors opportunities prevents them from becoming experienced stars. He also examines appeals to artistic freedom and nontraditional casting that obscured economic interests. [Ito, “A Certain Slant”](https://brightlightsfilm.com/certain-slant-brief-history-hollywood-yellowface/).

### 23. How did nineteenth-century commentators describe Chinese music?

**Provisional:** Euro-American commentators often dismissed it as noise, strange, shrill, or lacking qualities they associated with proper music. The key distinction is between unfamiliar conventions and supposedly inferior people: their descriptions turned the first into evidence of the second. **The exact assigned Moon chapter is needed to verify her specific examples and terminology.** [Moon, *Yellowface*, publisher description](https://www.rutgersuniversitypress.org/yellowface/9780813583631).

### 24. How does Moon connect musical judgments to Orientalism?

**Provisional application:** Treating European musical conventions as universal makes Chinese difference appear deficient. This reproduces an Orientalist hierarchy in which Western observers define civilization and classify others. Music criticism can therefore contribute to racial exclusion, rather than merely express taste. Moon's publisher confirms that the book analyzes music, performance, and Chinese racial stereotyping; her assigned chapter is needed for its detailed argument. [Moon, publisher](https://www.rutgersuniversitypress.org/yellowface/9780813583631).

### 25. Why yellowface and stereotyping, and what do they reveal politically?

This repeats Question 21. **Use the same mechanisms, then connect them:** white control over casting restricts who can speak; profitable stock characters restrict what can be said; repeated images make those restrictions seem natural. The political issue is who can define a racial group and benefit from that definition. [Ito](https://brightlightsfilm.com/certain-slant-brief-history-hollywood-yellowface/).

### 26. How does yellowface naturalize stereotypes and reveal race as performance?

**Makeup, accents, costume, and gestures manufacture a supposedly recognizable Asian type.** Repetition encourages viewers to mistake these conventions for natural traits. A villain and an obedient minority may look opposite, yet both deny ordinary individuality and belonging. Yellowface shows racial meaning being staged; it does not mean racism's consequences are imaginary or that racial identity is freely interchangeable. [Lee, introduction transcription, pp. 2–3](https://www.scribd.com/document/369791653/Orientals-Asian-Americans-in-Popular-Culture-by-Robert-G-Lee1375-Ch1).

## Week 4 — Racial Construction and *The Mask of Fu Manchu*

### 27. How do Dhingra/Rodriguez and Ono/Pham explain racial construction and persistence?

**Media attach racial meanings to people, then make those meanings familiar through repetition.** Ono and Pham emphasize their reproduction across institutions and media. The guide's quotation from Dhingra and Rodriguez shows racial and sexual hierarchies intersecting: Asian men become threats to white women. New media can inherit the same assumptions. **Dhingra/Rodriguez portion is provisional pending their assigned chapter.** [Ono and Pham, Chapter 2, pp. 25–28](https://asame30.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2017/02/ono-and-pham-ch-2.pdf).

### 28. How does the film portray Asian men as a sexual threat to white women?

**Sheila becomes a prize in a racial conquest fantasy.** Fu Manchu displays her to his followers and connects taking women like her with conquering the white world. This makes protecting white womanhood a justification for defeating the Asian villain. Separately, Fah Lo See's coercion of Terry extends the film's fear of interracial sexuality, although that example concerns an Asian woman and a white man. **Interpretation based on the documented plot and dialogue.** [AFI catalog](https://catalog.afi.com/Catalog/moviedetails/5880); [dialogue transcript](https://www.springfieldspringfield.co.uk/movie_script.php?movie=the-mask-of-fu-manchu).

### 29. What preferred message is encoded? Did most 1930s audiences accept it?

**A plausible preferred reading is that Asian power threatens Western civilization and the British heroes restore legitimate order.** A dominant reading accepts that frame; an oppositional one could see imperial fear and racist caricature. **We cannot establish what most 1930s audiences thought from the film alone.** Existing prejudices make acceptance plausible, but a majority claim requires reception evidence. [AFI, plot and production history](https://catalog.afi.com/Catalog/moviedetails/5880); [Hall](https://spkb.blot.im/_readings/EncodingDecoding_HALL_1980.pdf).

### 30. Which images and plot elements reproduce Yellow Peril?

**The story turns Asian political power into a threat to the white world.** Fu Manchu seeks Genghis Khan's relics to rally followers; hidden laboratories, torture, and exoticized settings connect Asia with sinister knowledge. The threatened white heroine makes racial conflict personal. The British expedition's entitlement to the relics largely escapes the suspicion directed at Fu Manchu. That final contrast is an analytical reading. [AFI](https://catalog.afi.com/Catalog/moviedetails/5880); [film transcript](https://www.springfieldspringfield.co.uk/movie_script.php?movie=the-mask-of-fu-manchu).

### 31. Does Fah Lo Suee reinforce or challenge the Dragon Lady?

**She reinforces it through sexual desire, power, and cruelty.** Her treatment of the captive Terry presents female agency as predatory. She challenges the image of a passive Asian woman, but the film makes her assertiveness frightening rather than liberating. Contrasting her with Sheila links Asian female sexuality to danger and white femininity to rescue. **Interpretation of documented plot details.** Sources use variant spellings, including Fah Lo See. [AFI](https://catalog.afi.com/Catalog/moviedetails/5880); [film transcript](https://www.springfieldspringfield.co.uk/movie_script.php?movie=the-mask-of-fu-manchu).

### 32. What are Yellow Peril discourse's historical origins and development?

**Ono and Pham connect it to older Western invasion fears and its broad circulation in the late nineteenth century.** U.S. media racialized Chinese immigration as danger; later political conflicts redirected the threat toward other Asian groups. The targets changed while the underlying Asian-threat/white-vulnerability structure endured. [Ono and Pham, pp. 28–30 and later historical examples](https://asame30.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2017/02/ono-and-pham-ch-2.pdf).

### 33. What characterizes Yellow Peril, and how did it shape immigration policy?

**It portrays Asians as invasive threats and whites as endangered.** This helped legitimate Chinese exclusion. The 1882 Chinese Exclusion Act suspended Chinese labor immigration and denied Chinese naturalization. [Ono and Pham](https://asame30.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2017/02/ono-and-pham-ch-2.pdf); [National Archives, law and historical explanation](https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/chinese-exclusion-act).

### 34. Does media racial hegemony still exist? Give an argument and example.

**A defensible answer is yes, as unequal influence rather than total control.** Consider the contrast between media portrayals of Asian success and continuing assumptions of foreignness. Pew's 2023 research documents both stereotypes in people's experiences. This supports the relevance of the framework, although it does not by itself prove media caused those experiences. Asian American creators and critical audiences also contest dominant meanings. [Pew, original survey research](https://www.pewresearch.org/race-and-ethnicity/2023/11/30/discrimination-experiences-shape-most-asian-americans-lives/).

### 35. Does globalization bring people together or exoticize differences?

**Possible argument: both; connection does not automatically produce equality or understanding.** Applying the passage supplied in the study guide, a globally distributed film can make a culture familiar while reducing it to spectacle. Translation, dialogue, and self-representation can also challenge stereotypes. Ask who selects the images, whose perspective explains them, and whether people appear as complex subjects. The answer depends on the terms of contact, not simply its quantity. **This is an argument responding to the supplied quotation.**

## Week 5 — Yellowface, National Belonging, and Asian American Women

### 36. How do Lee and Ono/Pham explain Yellow Peril's persistence?

**Racial images adapt to changing crises rather than simply disappearing.** Ono and Pham trace persistent threat discourse; Lee connects images to historically changing social conflicts. Their combined implication is that newer portrayals may update the surface while preserving exclusion. [Ono and Pham](https://asame30.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2017/02/ono-and-pham-ch-2.pdf); [Lee, publisher overview](https://tupress.temple.edu/books/orientals).

### 37. How do stereotypes define national belonging?

**They make Asian appearance signify foreign allegiance regardless of citizenship or birthplace.** Such portrayals set the boundaries of who counts as American. The problem therefore exceeds inaccurate description: a stereotype can make suspicion, exclusion, or unequal treatment appear justified. This is an application of the readings' account of racial alienness. [Lee, publisher overview](https://tupress.temple.edu/books/orientals); [Pew, foreigner stereotype findings](https://www.pewresearch.org/2023/11/30/asian-americans-and-the-forever-foreigner-stereotype/).

### 38. What is “alien” versus “foreign,” and how does it connect to the model minority?

**In Lee's distinction, foreign suggests external distance; alien describes a supposedly foreign presence within the nation.** An immigrant can thus be framed as an internal threat. Applying this to the model minority, economic usefulness does not necessarily confer full belonging: praise can coexist with suspicion. Success becomes conditional acceptance rather than an end to racialization. [Lee, introduction, p. 3](https://www.scribd.com/document/369791653/Orientals-Asian-Americans-in-Popular-Culture-by-Robert-G-Lee1375-Ch1); [Pew, model-minority research](https://www.pewresearch.org/race-and-ethnicity/2023/11/30/asian-americans-and-the-model-minority-stereotype/).

### 39. What is Yellow Peril, and what are Lee's six images?

**Yellow Peril imagines Asians as a collective threat to white national, economic, and racial order.** Terminology correction: Lee's six images are images of “the Oriental”; **Yellow Peril is one of the six**, rather than the name for all six.

| Image | Central racial construction |
|---|---|
| Pollutant | A contaminating presence |
| Coolie | Servile labor threatening white workers |
| Deviant | A threat to sexual and domestic norms |
| Yellow Peril | A threat to white national survival |
| Model Minority | Apparently successful assimilation serving dominant interests |
| Gook | A racialized enemy or subversive |

These are historical constructions, not traits of Asian people. [Lee, introduction, pp. 9–11](https://www.scribd.com/document/369791653/Orientals-Asian-Americans-in-Popular-Culture-by-Robert-G-Lee1375-Ch1); [publisher confirmation of the six](https://tupress.temple.edu/books/orientals).

### 40. What does persistent Yellow Peril reveal about representation's politics?

**Media can reproduce exclusion even when characters and formats change.** Ask whether a new economic rival or security villain revives an older racial threat. Persistence directs attention to the institutions that circulate images and the unequal power to define national danger. [Ono and Pham](https://asame30.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2017/02/ono-and-pham-ch-2.pdf).

### 41. How does yellowface contribute to racialization?

**It turns diverse people into a recognizable racial type.** Visual and behavioral conventions make constructed difference appear inherent. Yellowface therefore helps produce the racial meanings it seems merely to depict. [Lee, introduction, pp. 2–3](https://www.scribd.com/document/369791653/Orientals-Asian-Americans-in-Popular-Culture-by-Robert-G-Lee1375-Ch1).

### 42. What is yellowface's primary function?

**To mark Asians as fundamentally and permanently alien.** Lee's emphasis is on racial difference and exclusion from the national norm, rather than simply on a failure of realistic makeup. [Lee, introduction, p. 3](https://www.scribd.com/document/369791653/Orientals-Asian-Americans-in-Popular-Culture-by-Robert-G-Lee1375-Ch1).

### 43. How does popular culture shape perceptions of Asian Americans?

**It manufactures and circulates racial categories rather than merely reflecting reality.** Lee connects changing images to historical crises and dominant interests. Apparently flattering and openly hostile stereotypes can both preserve Asian alienness. [Lee, publisher overview](https://tupress.temple.edu/books/orientals).

### 44. How did *Ozawa* and *Thind* define race and whiteness?

**Both excluded Asian applicants, but the Court shifted its reasoning.** In *Ozawa v. United States* (1922), Ozawa emphasized light skin and American assimilation. The Court treated statutory whiteness as Caucasian racial classification, excluding Japanese people. In *United States v. Bhagat Singh Thind* (1923), Thind invoked the classification of high-caste Indians as Caucasian. The Court instead relied on the ordinary understanding of white people and excluded him.

**Analytical significance:** whiteness functioned as a flexible legal boundary protecting racial membership, rather than a consistent scientific or skin-color standard. The guide's 1922 citation belongs to *Ozawa*; *Thind* was decided in 1923. [Official *Ozawa* opinion](https://www.loc.gov/item/usrep260178/); [official *Thind* opinion](https://www.loc.gov/item/usrep261204/).

### 45. How does *Slaying the Dragon* connect colonialism and portrayals of Asian women?

**Provisional film synthesis:** Western power supplies the setting in which Asian women become exotic objects, servants, or romantic rewards. The documentary's official companion guide specifically links the white male hero and Asian female character to U.S. military presence in Asia. A colonial reading asks how unequal relations become stories of feminine submission or rescue. [Companion study guide, original-film section, PDF p. 2](https://www.wmm.com/uploads/2019/02/dragon_study.pdf).

### 46. What impact do stereotypes have on real Asian American women?

**Provisional:** Images of submissiveness or sexual availability can encourage fetishization and disregard for boundaries; the Dragon Lady can make assertiveness seem threatening. They also restrict professional and acting opportunities. The companion guide explicitly asks how the women interviewed describe effects on everyday life. **The full film or class notes are needed for a specific interviewee's testimony.** [Original-film companion guide](https://www.wmm.com/uploads/2019/02/dragon_study.pdf); [Columbia film synopsis](https://www.columbia.edu/cu/weai/exeas/films/slaying-dragon.html).

### 47. How do Orientalism, militarism, and gendered racial domination intertwine?

**Provisional analytical synthesis:** Orientalism makes Asian difference seem exotic; militarism establishes unequal encounters; gendered stereotypes make domination appear natural or romantic. In a rescue narrative, a Western man gains agency while an Asian woman's desires and circumstances become secondary. The documentary's military-presence/white-hero connection supports this approach; verify particular film clips against the screening. [Original-film companion guide, PDF p. 2](https://www.wmm.com/uploads/2019/02/dragon_study.pdf).

## Week 6 — The “Asian Mystique,” Dragon Lady, and Lotus Blossom

### 1. How was the Asian mystique produced through imperial encounters?

**Western encounters and storytelling turned Asian women into fantasies of exotic difference and availability.** Prasso's interview connects traders, missionaries, and unequal power to these images. The point is that the mystique reflects Western expectations rather than a shared nature of Asian women. [Prasso, recorded interview and transcript](https://americanarchive.org/catalog/cpb-aacip-16-z892805n9b).

### 2. How did historical accounts and popular culture produce the Dragon Lady?

**Provisional chapter answer:** Repeated stories attached mystery, sexual danger, and manipulation to Asian women. The named Dragon Lady in *Terry and the Pirates* helped give this fantasy a recognizable popular-cultural form. Distinguish the term's comic-strip history from the broader, older Orientalist pattern. Prasso's interview supports the pattern but does not establish the term's origin. [Smithsonian, Caniff's Dragon Lady](https://www.americanhistory.si.edu/collections/object/nmah_1400088); [Prasso interview](https://americanarchive.org/catalog/cpb-aacip-16-z892805n9b).

### 3. How did writers such as Marco Polo contribute to Western fantasies?

**Provisional:** Travel stories presented Asia as marvelous, wealthy, mysterious, and sexually different. Later audiences could treat selective descriptions as knowledge about an entire region. The archived interview's introduction traces this tradition to Marco Polo, but the accessible discussion does not verify Prasso's specific examples from his writing. **Her chapter is needed for those details.** [Archived interview framing](https://americanarchive.org/catalog/cpb-aacip-16-z892805n9b).

### 4. How did Victorian sexuality contrast with perceptions of Asian women?

**In Prasso's account, respectable English women's sexual expression was restricted while Asian women were imagined outside those restrictions.** This allowed Western men to fantasize about sexual freedom and submission abroad. She relates the contrast to traders' encounters and imperial dominance. This describes a Western projection, not Asian women's actual uniform behavior. [Prasso interview](https://americanarchive.org/catalog/cpb-aacip-16-z892805n9b).

### 5. How did portrayals of Empress Dowager Tzu Hsi shape the Dragon Lady?

**Provisional; assigned chapter needed.** A likely answer is that sensational Western portrayals turned a powerful Chinese woman into an emblem of cruelty, intrigue, and sexual danger, making female authority appear racially threatening. Tzu Hsi is also called Cixi. **This is a working interpretation of the study guide's premise; I could not verify Prasso's passage or its historical examples.** Do not treat it as a confirmed account of Cixi's conduct or the origin of the term.

### 6. How does the Dragon Lady stereotype depict Asian American women?

**As powerful, calculating, sexually manipulative, and dangerous.** Her agency becomes a threat to Western men rather than evidence of ordinary individuality. The stereotype makes an Asian woman's strength seem inherently sinister. [Prasso, book description](https://books.google.com/books/about/The_Asian_Mystique.html?id=0elZBgAAQBAJ).

### 7. Why does the Dragon Lady image persist?

**Provisional chapter answer:** Familiar images can be recycled across entertainment and everyday expectations. The trope provides a quick shorthand for exotic danger and female power, leaving little room for varied lives. Prasso's interview follows the broader mystique into contemporary screen culture; the assigned chapter is needed if your instructor expects one particular reason or example. [Prasso interview](https://americanarchive.org/catalog/cpb-aacip-16-z892805n9b).

### 8. What is the Lotus Blossom, and how does it contrast with the Dragon Lady?

**The Lotus Blossom is passive, delicate, obedient, and self-sacrificing; the Dragon Lady is assertive, controlling, and threatening.** They are opposing fantasies, but both define Asian women through others' desires and deny complexity. **Concept verified; Prasso's exact chapter wording remains unchecked.** [Asian Women United companion guide, polarized portrayals](https://www.wmm.com/uploads/2019/02/dragon_study.pdf); [Prasso book description](https://books.google.com/books/about/The_Asian_Mystique.html?id=0elZBgAAQBAJ).

### 9. What does *Picturing Oriental Girls* show about representation?

**Provisional, based on production and catalog descriptions:** Valerie Soe's found-footage video juxtaposes recurring images of Asian women with language from sexualized commercial texts. Repetition makes the stereotypes visible as a manufactured system across entertainment and commerce. An analytical takeaway is that these images organize expectations rather than innocently reflecting women's lives. **Full-film examples need checking against your screening.** [Soe's production interview](https://mediaburn.org/videos/women-of-vision-valerie-soe/); [film catalog](https://search.worldcat.org/title/Picturing-oriental-girls-%3A-a-RE-educational-videotape/oclc/28132367).
