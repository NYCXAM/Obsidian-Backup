# WEEK 1 — MEDIA AND CULTURAL THEORY

### 1. According to Said, what role does Orientalism play in Western culture?
**Source: Edward Said, *Orientalism***

Orientalism gives Western culture a system for **describing, studying, judging, and exercising authority over the Orient**. The West constructs the East as different from itself, and that contrast also strengthens Western identity: Europe can imagine itself as rational, civilized, modern, and powerful by constructing the Orient as its opposite. Said explicitly calls Orientalism a Western style for “dominating, restructuring, and having authority over the Orient.”

**Memorize:**  
**Orientalism = constructing the East + defining the West + exercising power.**

### 2. What does “Orientalism” primarily refer to? How/why is the framework used by the West?
**Source: Said**

Said gives Orientalism **three connected meanings**:

1. An **academic field** studying “the Orient.”
2. A **style of thought** based on a distinction between the “Orient” and “Occident.”
3. A **Western discourse/institution for dealing with the Orient**—describing it, teaching about it, making statements about it, and exercising authority over it.

The third meaning is especially important because it shows Orientalism is not merely having stereotypes. It is a whole system of **knowledge connected to power**.

### 3. How does Said’s Orientalism help us analyze Asian Americans in media?
**Source: Said — not Ono & Pham**

It tells us to ask how media **constructs** Asians rather than assuming the representation simply reflects reality. If Asian characters are repeatedly shown as mysterious, exotic, irrational, submissive, or dangerous, we should ask how those images define an Asian “Other” against a supposedly normal Western identity and **who has the authority to create those meanings**.

Said also makes clear that the actual cultures and people of the East have a reality far greater than what Western representations say about them.

**Key exam idea:** A stereotype is not only inaccurate; it can support a **West/East power relationship**.

# WEEK 2 — CULTURE, IDEOLOGY, HEGEMONY, REPRESENTATION

### 4. Why do Kellner and Durham say cultural texts are not neutral?
**Source: Kellner & Durham**

Because cultural texts contain **meanings, values, biases, and messages** connected to social power. Even something made as entertainment can contain representations of race, gender, sexuality, class, etc. that either reproduce or challenge existing relations of domination.

Their key phrase is:

**“There are no innocent texts.”**

### 5. According to Gramsci, what is the role of hegemony?
**Source: Antonio Gramsci**

Hegemony is a way a dominant group maintains power through **“intellectual and moral leadership”**—getting other groups to accept its leadership and worldview—not simply through physical force.

Gramsci distinguishes:

**Domination = coercion/force**  
**Hegemony = leadership/consent**

### 6. How have ruling-class ideologies changed over time?
**Best source: Kellner & Durham**

The specific ideology changes with the social system. Kellner and Durham give examples:

**Feudal society:** piety, honor, valor, military chivalry.  
**Capitalist society:** individualism, profit, competition, the market.  
**Contemporary global capitalism:** globalization, digital technologies, freer markets.

So dominant ideology **changes with historical conditions**, but ruling groups continue making their particular values appear natural or universal.

### 7. Does ideology operate more strongly in mass film than fine art?
**Source/framework: Gramsci**

Gramsci does **not explicitly rank film versus fine art**. His point is that ideology operates through a huge network of institutions and cultural spaces.

The press is especially dynamic, but he also names **schools, libraries, associations, clubs, architecture, and even street layouts/names**.

So an exam answer could say mass film might have **greater reach**, but Gramsci’s framework suggests ideology is present throughout civil society, not only in popular mass media.

### 8. How does Gramsci’s understanding of ideology change how we think about media?
**Source: Gramsci**

Ideology is not simply a lie that people consciously believe. Gramsci says historically important ideologies **organize people and create the terrain on which they understand their position and struggle**.

Therefore, media can help build the very “common sense” through which people interpret society. Media becomes part of the **material structure of ideology**, not merely entertainment.

### 9. Hall’s three hypothetical decoding positions
**Source: Stuart Hall, “Encoding/Decoding”**

**Dominant-hegemonic:** The viewer understands the preferred meaning and accepts it.

**Negotiated:** The viewer broadly accepts the dominant framework but modifies it according to their own circumstances.

**Oppositional:** The viewer understands the preferred meaning but deliberately interprets it through an opposing framework.

**Easy memory:**  
**Dominant = agree**  
**Negotiated = mostly agree, but…**  
**Oppositional = understand + reject**

### 10. What is the flaw in the traditional communication model?
**Source: Hall**

The traditional model is too **linear**:

**sender → message → receiver**

Hall argues communication actually involves linked but distinct moments of **production, circulation, distribution/consumption, and reproduction**. What producers encode is not automatically identical to what audiences decode.

### 11. What role do “preferred meanings” play?
**Source: Hall**

Media messages can support multiple interpretations, but meanings are **not all equally powerful**. A society has dominant or “preferred” meanings shaped by institutional, political, and ideological structures.

Preferred meanings make one interpretation appear more **normal, legitimate, or common sense**, without completely determining what audiences think.

### 12. How does Hall challenge the idea that media messages have fixed meanings?
**Source: Hall**

Encoding and decoding are not identical. Audiences bring different social positions, experiences, and codes to a text, so they can accept, modify, or reject its preferred meaning.

Therefore:

**producer intention ≠ guaranteed audience meaning.**

### 13. How does Hall define the critique of representation?
**Source: Hall**

Important nuance: **Hall does not give a formal definition called “the critique of representation” in this excerpt.**

But his argument gives you the answer: media does not transparently copy reality. A real event has to be transformed into a **story/discourse using codes** before it can communicate. We therefore critique representation by examining **how reality was encoded, what signs and meanings were selected, what ideology those meanings contain, and how audiences might decode them differently**.

### 14. How does the ruling class primarily maintain cultural hegemony?
**Source: Gramsci**

Through **consent and intellectual/moral leadership**, supported by institutions of civil society, rather than relying solely on physical coercion.

### 15. How does hegemony explain persistent systemic inequality?
**Source: Gramsci**

Inequality can survive even in a society that officially values equality because dominant ideas become embedded in **ordinary institutions and common sense**.

The press, schools, churches, organizations, cultural institutions, and even the built environment continuously reinforce particular understandings of society.

**Key idea:** If unequal arrangements feel “normal,” people do not need to be forced to accept them.

# WEEK 3 — HOLLYWOOD’S IMAGINED ORIENT / YELLOWFACE

### 16. According to Feng, what does “Asian” mean in England?
**Source: Peter Feng**

In England, “Asian” generally means **Pakistani and/or Indian—South Asian**.

Feng contrasts that with the United States, where “Asian” commonly refers more heavily to **East and Southeast Asians**.

**Point:** “Asian” is not a natural, universal category. Its meaning is historically and geographically constructed.

### 17. How does Feng’s Asian American cinema challenge Hollywood’s construction of Asians as racial objects?
**Sources: Feng + Benshoff & Griffin**

Benshoff and Griffin show Hollywood historically created a generalized **“Oriental”** through costume, makeup, accent, and stereotyped behavior, while actual Asian characters were marginalized and white actors received major Asian roles.

Feng instead centers **Asian American filmmakers, perspectives, self-definition, and diversity**. Asian Americans become subjects who produce meaning instead of merely racial objects defined by Hollywood.

### 18. How does Feng define Asian American cinema? Do you agree?
**Source: Feng**

Feng deliberately shows that there is **no simple final definition**.

Possible definitions include:

- films made by Asian American filmmakers;
- films about Asian American subjects;
- films made through an Asian American perspective;
- a historically specific Asian American filmmaking movement.

He warns that defining it only by content can create one supposedly **“correct” Asian American experience**.

Judi Nihei gives one narrower version: **Asian American stories through the eyes of Asian American filmmakers.**

**Possible opinion:** A flexible definition makes more sense because Asian American communities and filmmakers are too diverse to fit one content formula.

### 19. How does Feng define the critique of representation?
**Source: Feng**

Again, Feng does **not present a neat formal definition with that exact label**.

His article critiques the way critics and markets reduce Asian American filmmaking to ethnicity. Asian American filmmakers may be judged mainly on whether their work looks “Asian American,” while white filmmakers get discussed in terms of **individual style and artistic technique**.

So the critique is not merely “is this representation positive?” It asks **who gets categorized, who defines the category, what the category allows artists to do, and what gets erased by it**.

Feng notes that the label can provide grants and exhibition opportunities while still being **“imposed from the outside.”**

### 20. How did yellowface limit Asian American actors?
**Sources: Benshoff & Griffin + Robert B. Ito**

Asian actors were commonly confined to supporting stereotypes such as **houseboys, cooks, laundry workers, servants, sidekicks, or villains**, while major Asian roles were given to white stars.

Ito calls this a **catch-22**: Asian actors supposedly lacked experience, but they could not gain experience because major Asian roles went to white actors.

### 21. Why did Hollywood use yellowface and stereotypes? What does it reveal?
**Sources: Ito + Benshoff & Griffin + Said**

Major reasons included:

- protecting jobs and starring roles for white actors;
- believing audiences preferred white stars;
- claiming there were no “qualified” Asian actors;
- the existing Orientalist expectation that an Asian character should look or behave a certain stereotypical way;
- commercial interests and the star system.

Ito even notes white actors were said to make better **“Orientals”** because they performed the stereotype audiences expected.

**Politics of representation:** the dominant industry controlled both **who could work** and **what “Asian” was supposed to look like**.

### 22. According to Ito, how was white casting justified?
**Source: Ito only**

Ito gives several justifications:

**“Giving the audience what they want.”**  
**There were supposedly no qualified Asian actors.**  
**White actors supposedly made better “Orientals.”**

In *Miss Saigon*, **“artistic freedom”** was invoked, but Ito argues that it largely disguised an economic decision involving an extremely profitable production.

### 23. According to Moon, how was Chinese music described?
**Source: Krystyn Moon**

European and American commentators frequently treated Chinese music as **noise, unmusical, primitive, or evidence of cultural inferiority**.

Moon says Euro-American writers created racial distinctions between **“music” and “noise”** and questioned whether Chinese people were capable of participating in Western music.

### 24. How does Moon connect that to Orientalism?
**Source: Moon**

Westerners treated their own musical rules as the standard of civilization. Because Chinese traditions differed, they were judged deficient rather than understood on their own terms.

Moon directly connects this to Said: Orientalism places the imagined Orient in opposition to the West and helps establish Western authority. European and American observers ignored the actual diversity of Chinese musical traditions and portrayed China as lacking real music.

### 25. Why did Hollywood use yellowface/stereotyping? Synthesize Week 3 and earlier readings.
**Sources: Said + Benshoff & Griffin + Ito + Moon**

This is the big synthesis:

**Said:** Orientalism supplied an already-existing West/East power framework.

**Moon:** performance helped turn racial difference into recognizable sounds, accents, costumes, postures, and images.

**Benshoff & Griffin:** Hollywood reduced diverse Asian peoples into stock Oriental characters.

**Ito:** economic interests and exclusion of Asian actors maintained the practice.

So yellowface was not simply bad makeup—it was a **system of representation, labor exclusion, and racial power**.

### 26. What does yellowface tell us about race as performance rather than biology?
**Sources: Moon + Ito + Benshoff & Griffin**

Yellowface literally manufactures the “Asian” body using **makeup, prosthetic eyes, skin coloring, accents, posture, clothing, and gestures**.

Moon specifically notes that dialect, makeup, posture, and costuming combined to mark the Chinese body as **inferior and foreign**.

That exposes a contradiction: if racial identity has to be **performed and manufactured**, then the supposedly “natural” racial traits are themselves cultural constructions.

# WEEK 4 — RACIAL CONSTRUCTION / *THE MASK OF FU MANCHU*

### 27. Dhingra & Rodriguez + Ono & Pham: how does media construct and naturalize race?
**Sources: Dhingra & Rodriguez, “The media and representations of race” + Ono & Pham Introduction**

Dhingra and Rodriguez say media can act as **teachers of values, ideologies, and beliefs**. Media can disempower groups through negative portrayals **and through failing to portray them at all**; it can confer status, and “controlling images” can make racial hierarchy appear normal.

Ono and Pham add the historical part: representations leave **residual effects**. Even when media changes, older representational templates continue setting the boundaries for later images unless the structures of production themselves change.

Their “Responses to mainstream media” reading adds that Asian Americans can create alternative/self-representations, but there is **no single Asian American voice**, and alternative representation does not automatically escape dominant media expectations.

### 28. Dhingra & Rodriguez’s sexual Yellow Peril + *Mask of Fu Manchu*
**Source: Dhingra & Rodriguez + film**

Dhingra and Rodriguez say yellow peril includes the fear that Asian men threaten **white women sexually**, while Asian women are portrayed as deceptive sexual temptresses or Dragon Ladies.

*The Mask of Fu Manchu* develops this directly. Fu Manchu’s racial conquest includes possessing white women, culminating in his call for his followers to kill white men and take their women. Fah Lo See also sexualizes and threatens a white male captive.

So the film makes racial conquest simultaneously a **military and sexual/reproductive threat**.

### 29. Hall + *Mask*: what is the preferred meaning?
**Source: Hall + film**

A likely preferred meaning is:

**Fu Manchu/the Oriental world represents a dangerous threat, while the white Western heroes represent the civilization that must defeat and contain it.**

Because 1930s audiences already lived within widespread Orientalist and yellow-peril cultural codes, many viewers were likely positioned to decode that message dominantly.

But Hall’s key qualification is important: **preferred does not mean guaranteed**. Audiences could still negotiate or oppose that reading.

### 30. How does *Mask* reproduce yellow peril?
**Source: film, using yellow-peril concept from course readings**

Specific elements:

- Fu Manchu wants the Genghis Khan artifacts to unite **Asia against the West**.
- Asians are treated as a collective mass threatening Western civilization.
- Fu Manchu is mysterious, technologically/scientifically dangerous, cruel, and untrustworthy.
- Exotic torture and settings turn the Orient into a place of barbarity and mystery.
- The threat extends to **white women and racial reproduction**.
- White Western characters are positioned as defenders of civilization.

That is basically the yellow-peril formula: **Asian takeover + vulnerable West**.

### 31. Fah Lo See and the Dragon Lady
**Source: film**

She strongly reinforces the stereotype: she is **beautiful, sexually aggressive, powerful, sadistic, exotic, and dangerous**.

She has agency, so she is not a passive woman, but the film turns that agency into evidence of her danger.

**Key contradiction:** Asian female power appears, but it is made threatening.

### 32. Ono & Pham: origins/development of yellow peril
**Source: Ono & Pham, “Persistence of Yellow Peril Discourse”**

They say the conceptual framework predates modern U.S. racism: scholars trace versions back to **Greek fears of Persia** and medieval fears of **Genghis Khan/Mongol invasion**.

It spreads broadly in the late nineteenth century around Chinese immigration, combining **racial fear, sexual anxiety, invasion, disease, moral inferiority, and fears of Western civilization being overwhelmed**.

It then changes targets depending on historical conditions: Chinese immigrants, wartime Japanese, economic Japan, China, Asian gangs, etc.

### 33. What is yellow peril’s main characteristic, and how did it affect immigration policy?
**Source: Ono & Pham**

Their basic definition:

Asians/Asian Americans are represented as threatening to **take over, invade, or negatively “Asianize”** the United States, while whites are portrayed as vulnerable.

That discourse helped justify anti-Chinese immigration policy, including the **Angell Treaty of 1880, Chinese Exclusion Act of 1882, and later exclusion laws**.

### 34. Does media racial hegemony still exist?
**Source: Ono & Pham Introduction**

A strong answer is **yes, but it is contested rather than absolute**.

Ono and Pham define media racial hegemony as media’s role in **both continuing and contesting racial and colonial power relations**. Media representations help guide ideas and behavior about race, often without audiences consciously noticing.

Examples from their own reading include repeated typecasting, model-minority imagery, yellowface logic, and yellow peril. Independent Asian American media and activism can challenge this hegemony.

### 35. Has globalization brought people together or separated them?
**Source: Ono & Pham Introduction**

Best answer: **both are possible.**

Technology creates more communication and an **illusion of proximity**, but increased exposure does not guarantee contextual understanding. Global media can actually make outsiders appear more exotic, strange, or alien and emphasize difference.

So:

**more information ≠ automatically more understanding.**

# WEEK 5 — YELLOWFACE / GENDER / *SLAYING THE DRAGON*

### 36. Ono & Pham + Lee: how has yellow peril persisted?
**Sources: Ono & Pham + Robert G. Lee**

Ono and Pham show that the **specific target changes while the structure survives**. Yellow peril moves from Chinese immigration to wartime Japan, economic competition with Japan, fears about China, and Asian criminal/gangster imagery.

Lee similarly argues that racial images are **historically unstable and contradictory**: Asians can move between images of threat, servility, deviance, success, and enemy depending on the social crisis.

So persistence does **not mean the exact same stereotype repeats forever**. It means the underlying image of Asians as an **alien threat to the American nation** keeps being adapted.

### 37. Lee + Ono/Pham: perpetual foreigners and “real Americans”
**Sources: Robert G. Lee + Ono & Pham**

Lee says popular culture helps determine **who counts as a “real American.”** Citizenship and cultural belonging are not automatically the same: Asian Americans can legally belong to the nation while still being represented as aliens.

Ono and Pham similarly argue that Orientalism and yellow peril construct Asians and Asian Americans as **perpetual foreigners and ever-present threats**.

Therefore, stereotypes do more than insult people—they define the symbolic boundary of **American national belonging**.

### 38. Alien vs. foreign and the model minority
**Source: Robert G. Lee**

Lee makes an important distinction:

**Foreign:** outside or distant.  
**Alien:** physically present inside society but imagined as having a foreign nature or allegiance.

A tourist can be foreign without being threatening because they are expected to leave. An immigrant who stays can become an “alien” because they disturb the imagined boundary of the nation.

The model minority therefore does not eliminate foreignness. Asian Americans can be praised as successful, efficient, and assimilated while still being treated as **not fully authentic Americans**; their success can even become evidence of hidden foreign power or subversion.

### 39. Lee: what is yellow peril and what are the six faces?
**Source: Robert G. Lee**

Important correction to the study guide wording:

Lee actually calls them the **“Six Faces of the Oriental,” not six faces of yellow peril.** Yellow peril is **one of the six**.

They are:

1. **Pollutant** — Asian presence portrayed as contaminating the white nation.
2. **Coolie** — Asian laborer portrayed as servile/unfree and a threat to white workers.
3. **Deviant** — Asians portrayed as sexually/gender deviant and a threat to proper family life.
4. **Yellow peril** — Asians portrayed as an invading threat to race, family, and nation.
5. **Model minority** — Asians portrayed as obedient, successful, assimilating minorities.
6. **Gook** — Asians portrayed as faceless/invisible enemy, especially connected to U.S. wars in Asia.

Lee explicitly lists those six as images of the Oriental as an **alien body and threat to the American national family**.

### 40. What does yellow peril’s persistence reveal about representation?
**Sources: Lee + Ono & Pham**

It shows stereotypes are shaped by **political and historical needs**, not simply by observations of real people.

Ono and Pham define yellow peril as a **manufactured rather than actual threat**: representations can shift public understanding of Asians toward danger even when the supposed threat does not exist.

So representation helps society interpret immigration, war, economic competition, and national security through racial categories.

### 41. How does yellowface racialize Asian Americans?
**Source: Lee**

Yellowface takes selected bodily features—eye shape, skin color, teeth, etc.—and gives those features **racial meaning**.

Lee argues that race is not simply a natural category; society decides which physical differences count as racial markers. Yellowface then exaggerates those markers and makes the Oriental appear sharply different from whiteness.

### 42. What is yellowface’s primary function?
**Source: Lee**

To mark the Oriental as **racially different from whiteness and “indelibly alien.”**

Yellowface makes an Asian body into a visual sign of foreignness and turns cultural difference into something that appears physically permanent.

### 43. How does popular culture affect perceptions of Asian Americans?
**Source: Lee**

Lee treats popular culture as a major arena where society debates:

**What is America? Who belongs? Who is a “real American”?**

Popular culture therefore does not only entertain; it helps define the racial categories through which people understand national identity.

### 44. Ozawa and Thind
**Source: Robert G. Lee**

**Takao Ozawa v. United States (1922):** Ozawa emphasized his light skin and cultural assimilation. The Court still ruled that Japanese ancestry made him nonwhite. Actual skin color and assimilation did not override racial ancestry.

**United States v. Bhagat Singh Thind (1923):** Thind argued that high-caste Indians were scientifically classified as Aryan/Caucasian and therefore white. The Court rejected the scientific classification and instead relied on the ordinary **“common understanding”** that Indians were not white.

**Huge takeaway:** The two cases expose race as a **socially and politically defined category**, not a consistent biological or scientific one.

### 45. *Slaying the Dragon*: how did colonialism shape images of Asian women?
**Source: film**

Western colonial and military encounters placed white Western men in positions of greater power over Asian societies. Images of Asian women as **geishas, servants, prostitutes, submissive lovers, or sexually available women** grew out of those unequal encounters and were later repeated by Hollywood.

The political relationship:

**powerful West / subordinate East**

gets transformed into a gender relationship:

**dominant Western man / submissive Asian woman.**

### 46. What impact do Hollywood stereotypes have on real Asian American women?
**Source: film**

They shape real expectations about how Asian American women are supposed to behave—such as being **submissive, exotic, sexually available, quiet, obedient, or alternatively manipulative and dangerous**.

The documentary’s interviews show these images affecting **dating, workplace treatment, sexual attention, identity, and how other people interpret their behavior**.

### 47. How are Orientalism, militarism, and gender domination connected?
**Source: film**

Orientalism defines Asia as exotic and fundamentally different. U.S. militarism created unequal encounters with Asian societies, especially involving American servicemen. Hollywood then turned those unequal political relationships into gendered fantasies about Asian women.

So:

**Orientalism → Asia is exotic/different**  
**Militarism/colonial power → West dominates Asia**  
**Gender representation → Asian woman becomes submissive/available to Western man**

The Dragon Lady provides the other side: when the Asian woman has power or sexual agency, that power becomes **dangerous**.

# WEEK 6 — PRASSO / ASIAN WOMEN

### 1. How does Prasso show the “Asian mystique” came from Western imperial encounters?
**Source: Sheridan Prasso**

Prasso traces Western ideas about Asian women through **travelers, traders, missionaries, colonialists, soldiers, writers, and media**.

Those Westerners often encountered Asian women through unequal situations involving courtesans, servants, prostitutes, mistresses, or colonial subjects. Those limited experiences were generalized into claims that Asian women were naturally **exotic, submissive, mysterious, or sexually available**.

So the “Asian mystique” reflects the **conditions of Western contact and Western fantasy**, not ordinary Asian women’s actual lives.

### 2. What role did historical accounts and popular culture play? How did Dragon Lady emerge?
**Source: Prasso**

Travel writing, scholarship, fiction, newspapers, and later film repeated sensational ideas about Asia until they became culturally familiar.

Prasso’s clearest example is **Empress Dowager Tzu Hsi**. Fabricated accounts portrayed her as sexually depraved, evil, and murderous. Those stories were treated as factual history for decades and helped create the image of the powerful Asian woman as a **Dragon Lady**.

Even after the fraud was exposed, Prasso says the image had become effectively indelible.

### 3. How did Marco Polo contribute to Western fantasies?
**Source: Prasso**

Marco Polo returned with sensational stories portraying Asia as a place of **freely available sexuality**, including women supposedly offered to travelers and large numbers of women serving powerful rulers.

Prasso suggests some of these travelers’ tales were exaggerated and fed European desire for the **exotic, sexual, and mysterious**. Later writers such as Chaucer, Dante, and Columbus repeated or drew from Polo’s stories.

### 4. Victorian sexuality vs. views of Asian women
**Source: Prasso**

Victorian English women were expected to be sexually restrained—Prasso describes the expectation that respectable women should not even appear to enjoy sex.

Against that background, Western men imagined Asian women as knowing **“all the arts and wiles of love”** and capable of satisfying any sexual desire.

So Asian women may have been engaging in behavior we would now consider normal, but Victorian repression made that behavior appear extremely exotic and sexually liberated.

### 5. How did Tzu Hsi contribute to the Dragon Lady stereotype?
**Source: Prasso**

More precisely, **the Western portrayal of Tzu Hsi** created it.

Fabricated stories described her using sex, murder, intrigue, and cruelty. Those accounts were repeatedly cited until fiction became accepted as historical knowledge.

Prasso stresses that this distorted image—not the actual woman—became the template connecting **Asian female power with sexual depravity and danger**.

### 6. What is the Dragon Lady stereotype?
**Source: Prasso**

A powerful Asian woman portrayed as:

**seductive + mysterious + manipulative + sexually threatening + cruel/dangerous.**

The crucial part is that **female power itself becomes racialized and sexualized**. Rather than simply being a powerful woman, the Asian woman’s power becomes evidence that she is dangerous.

### 7. Why does Prasso say the Dragon Lady persists?
**Source: Prasso**

One reason is the **power of repeated cultural stories**. Once false accounts entered scholarship and popular memory, even exposing them as false did not erase the image.

The stereotype was also politically useful: portraying rulers such as Tzu Hsi as monstrous helped provide a **justification for Western actions in China**.

That is why the correction can be weaker than the original myth.

### 8. What is the Lotus Blossom stereotype, and how does it contrast with Dragon Lady?
**Best supporting source uploaded: Dhingra & Rodriguez + *Slaying the Dragon*; this is NOT primarily from the Prasso excerpt**

**Lotus Blossom:** submissive, obedient, passive, loyal, exotic, self-sacrificing, and ready to cater to men.

**Dragon Lady:** powerful, sexually aggressive, manipulative, dangerous, and threatening.

They seem opposite:

**Lotus Blossom = Asian woman has too little power**  
**Dragon Lady = Asian woman has “too much” power**

But both are Orientalist because both define Asian women through **Western expectations about gender and sexuality**.
