## Week 7: (Mis)representation of Asian American men in media

### 7.1

**According to Ono and Pham, how are Asian and Asian American men typically portrayed in U.S. media?**

Ono and Pham identify two recurring patterns. Asian men can appear as **Yellow Peril threats**: predatory villains, gangsters, or martial arts foes who supposedly threaten white society. They can also be **emasculated or desexualized**: geeky, socially awkward, unattractive, or sidelined as romantic partners. Long Duk Dong in *Sixteen Candles* is a useful example of comic feminization and foreignness. Both patterns center a white, heterosexual standard of masculinity. Even an apparently strong fighter may be denied romance, emotional depth, or a life outside fighting.

*Reading: Ono and Pham, ch. 4, PDF pp. 1–2, 7–9; ch. 2 on Yellow Peril.*

### 7.2

**What does the portrayal of Asian men as tyrannical, lecherous, and barbaric in U.S. media historically serve to justify?**

The barbaric-villain image gives **white male intervention a moral justification**. A story first casts Asian men as abusive toward Asian women and predatory toward white women. It can then present a white man as the natural rescuer and romantic partner, and the removal of the Asian man as deserved. Ono and Pham connect this to colonial narratives that present domination as civilization or protection. The rescue plot turns a hierarchy into an apparent act of benevolence.

*Reading: Ono and Pham, ch. 4, PDF pp. 1–2, 7.*

### 7.3

**What historical factors contributed to the stereotypical portrayal of Asian American men as asexual and emasculated in U.S. media?**

Connect **material exclusions** to the way media represented them. Immigration exclusion and barriers to bringing wives and families helped create some predominantly male immigrant communities. Racial barriers to employment pushed some Chinese immigrant men toward laundry, cooking, and domestic or service work, which dominant U.S. culture coded as feminine. Anti-miscegenation restrictions and racial hostility constrained courtship and marriage across racial lines. Popular media could then treat the results of exclusion as proof that Asian men were naturally weak, sexless, or unsuitable partners. The stereotype also managed anxiety about competition by making Asian men seem harmless or undesirable when stories did not cast them as threats.

*Reading: Ono and Pham, ch. 4, PDF pp. 7–10; ch. 2; Robert G. Lee, “Introduction: Yellowface,” for the historical racialization of Asian labor and sexuality.*

### 7.4

**According to Ono and Pham, what is the significance of using an intersectional approach to understand the media representations of Asian and Asian American gender and sexuality?**

**Intersectional analysis** asks how race, gender, and sexuality produce each other in a representation. Looking at gender alone would tell us that Asian men and women receive different roles. Looking at the roles together explains what they do: a story can make an Asian woman desirable to a white man by treating her race as exotic and her femininity as available, while making the Asian man a poor romantic rival. The white male lead thus comes to look both masculine and morally entitled to intervene. An intersectional reading also notices when attempts to “restore” Asian masculinity imply that femininity or queerness is inferior.

*Reading: Ono and Pham, ch. 4, PDF pp. 1–3, 7–10.*

### 7.5

**How do The Slanted Screen and Ono and Pham demonstrate that Asian American men and women have been represented differently yet interrelatedly in U.S. media? What historical and ideological forces have shaped these contrasting representations?**

Jeff Adachi’s *The Slanted Screen* shows how Hollywood limits Asian men through casting and repetition, from the danger attached to early star Sessue Hayakawa’s screen persona to the narrow space offered by martial arts or comic roles. Ono and Pham explain the other side of that pattern: the **Lotus Blossom** is coded as compliant and available, while the **Dragon Lady** is threatening through sexual agency. These images work together when a story elevates a white man as a suitable romantic partner or rescuer and sidelines Asian men. Colonial rescue fantasies, U.S. wars and military encounters in Asia, anxieties about interracial intimacy, and Hollywood casting conventions all help explain how the contrast became durable. *Sayonara* is a useful complication: it challenges some barriers to interracial marriage while retaining unequal ways of looking at Asian women.

*Reading and film: Ono and Pham, ch. 4, PDF pp. 1–8; [The Slanted Screen](https://catalog.afi.com/Film/64038-THE-SLANTED-SCREEN-ASIAN-MEN-IN-FILM-AND-TELEVISION?cxt=filmography).*

### 7.6

**Some of the interviewees in The Slanted Screen say that less racialized roles should be written and that this will open up roles for more diverse casting. Clearly this is the case, as many roles don't require a character to be a certain race to tell the story properly. This means more actors can take roles regardless of their race. However, sometimes race is often important to tell particular stories. In your opinion, in what situations might race be important to tell a fictional story and how do we know race is important for a story?**

**Sample position.** Race matters when it changes the character’s relationships, choices, or the institutions they face. In a story about Japanese American incarceration, racial classification is part of what causes the government to remove the family. In *Mississippi Masala*, Mina’s and Demetrius’s backgrounds shape their families’ reactions and what the romance reveals about Uganda and Mississippi. For a role such as a detective whose case never depends on ancestry, open casting adds opportunities without a major rewrite. A useful test is to ask which scenes, conflicts, and relationships would change if the character’s background changed. Even an open-cast role can include cultural specificity after casting.

*Reading and film: Davé, pp. 143–147; The Slanted Screen.*

### 7.7

**How can we make an innovative Asian American film in the face of the dominant discourse of “claiming America” and its problematic (yet understandable) desires for voice, visibility, and authenticity and “positive” representations?**

**Claiming America** is a demand for belonging and a response to erasure. It becomes limiting when films must prove that Asian Americans are respectable, give one supposedly authentic account, or replace every negative image with a positive one. One way forward is to give characters room for contradiction and let form carry part of the argument. In Wayne Wang’s *Chan Is Missing*, a search for a missing person yields competing accounts rather than a definitive explanation of Chinese American identity. Another route is Oishi’s example of *Love Song for Persis K.*, in which queer fandom becomes a way of making meaning from Hollywood images. Innovation comes from trying out ways of telling and seeing, along with expanding who gets to tell the story.

*Reading: Feng, “In Search of Asian American Cinema,” pp. 32–35; Tajima-Peña, “Defining a Framework for Asian American Cinema”; Oishi, pp. 223–227.*

### 7.8

**As discussed in The Slanted Screen, what are the strategic steps we can take to change the overall stereotypical representation of Asian American men in media in general? Do you agree with these steps?**

**Practical steps:** Write a broader range of roles and open casting where ethnicity is incidental. When race matters, give the role a specific history and relationships rather than relying on stock traits. Increase Asian American authority in writing, directing, producing, and casting, and support independent projects and their distribution. Adachi’s documentary gives context for these changes by showing how actors can face a choice between limited work and typecasting. **Sample position:** I support wider casting and greater creative control together. Open casting widens access, while creative control helps ensure actors get stories, relationships, and genres that previous gatekeepers rarely offered them. Success should include the freedom to play flawed people and ordinary people as well as heroes.

*Reading and film: The Slanted Screen; Davé, pp. 143–147; Tajima-Peña, “Entitlement.”*

## Week 8: The Model Minority Myth; Accent Racialization

### 8.1

**According to Ono and Pham, how does the "model minority" stereotype functions to create a divide between Asian Americans and other racial minorities?**

The model minority stereotype holds up selected Asian American achievements as evidence that hard work, family values, and compliance are sufficient for mobility. It implicitly tells Black, Latino, and other communities that unequal outcomes are their own fault. This **wedge** diverts attention from structural racism and differing histories of immigration, labor, and exclusion. It also offers Asian Americans conditional approval for accepting the existing order, weakening the basis for coalitions. William Petersen’s 1966 Japanese American success profile and the 1966 *U.S. News & World Report* profile of Chinese Americans are useful examples of the wider media discourse.

*Reading: Ono and Pham, ch. 5, PDF pp. 1–2; “U.S. News success story” course scan.*

### 8.2

**How do Ono and Pham explain the relationship between the model minority stereotype and yellow peril? How can media portrayals of Asian American success simultaneously celebrate achievement and construct Asian Americans as threats to the nation?**

Model minority and Yellow Peril can be **two readings of the same success**. Academic or economic achievement appears admirable when it supposedly proves that American institutions reward effort. The same achievement becomes threatening when media frame Asian people as foreign competitors taking jobs, school places, or control of the economy. Ono and Pham discuss *Rising Sun*, in which Japanese discipline and business skill help make Japan both a model competitor and a menace. This logic can collapse distinctions between Asian nations and Asian Americans, leaving U.S. belonging conditional even when people succeed.

*Reading: Ono and Pham, ch. 5, particularly the discussion of Kawai and Rising Sun, PDF pp. 8–14.*

### 8.3

**According to Ono and Pham, how does the "model minority" stereotype impact perceptions of Asian Americans?**

The stereotype encourages audiences to assume Asian Americans are uniformly educated, affluent, disciplined, and self-sufficient. It hides differences among ethnicities, immigration histories, and classes, making poverty, language barriers, workplace discrimination, and unmet educational needs easier to overlook. It can also narrow how individual people are perceived: an Asian doctor may be expected to excel technically while being read as unfeeling or lacking leadership qualities. There can be pressure to perform success and stay quiet about harm, while achievement itself can feed resentment.

*Reading: Ono and Pham, ch. 5, PDF pp. 1–8, 12–14. Compare the treatment of Deb Chen in ER and Cristina Yang in Grey’s Anatomy.*

### 8.4

**Why are Asian Americans overrepresented in the hard sciences and technical fields, such as engineering and computer science, and medical field? How much role does media representation of Asian Americans play in this choice?**

Some occupational patterns reflect **who could immigrate and the opportunities they found**. Immigration pathways favoring trained professionals, international recruitment, college and graduate-school pipelines, networks that help people enter professions, the economic appeal of stable careers, and family expectations can all contribute. This varies substantially by ancestry, migration history, field, and generation. A claim about engineering or physician roles should specify the comparison group and should not be generalized to all Asian Americans or all health occupations. Media may affect who young people see as a role model and what peers, employers, or counselors expect of them. The reading’s analysis of TV doctors does not measure how much media caused career choice.

*Reading and background: Ono and Pham, ch. 5, PDF pp. 3–8; [USCIS history of U.S. immigration preferences](https://www.uscis.gov/sites/default/files/document/fact-sheets/INSHistory.pdf); [National Science Board workforce data](https://ncses.nsf.gov/pubs/nsbsep20261/figure/11).*

### 8.5

**How does this overrepresentation bolster the model minority myth? What role does media play in perpetuating this stereotype?**

Visible concentration in certain jobs gives media a **selective fact** to turn into a group explanation. A network can repeatedly cast Asian Americans as scientists or doctors, give them few stories outside work, and encourage the audience to treat professional skill as an inborn racial quality. The evidence selected for coverage then seems to confirm the stereotype that shaped the selection. Ono and Pham discuss Deb Chen as a foil to John Carter in *ER* and note how Cristina Yang can be depicted as skilled but robotic, even though her character also has a rich personal life. These examples show why professional success and fuller representation are separate questions.

*Reading: Ono and Pham, ch. 5, PDF pp. 3–6.*

### 8.6

**According to Shilpa Davé, what is the concept of “brown voice”? How does the use of “brown voice” (accent racialization) create the discourse of orientalism or the “otherness”? Can you think of a film or TV show where a character’s accent shapes how you perceive their identity. Does the accent empower the character or reduce them to a type? What does this reveal about how race is “performed” through voice?**

Davé uses **brown voice** for the performance of accented English marked as Indian or South Asian, through cues such as stress, pronunciation, syntax, and cultural references. South Asian and non-South Asian performers can both produce it. It racializes by making a voice stand for foreignness, even where the viewer cannot see the speaker. It echoes Orientalism when a dominant U.S. perspective treats an internally diverse region as a familiar, comic or exotic type. Apu in *The Simpsons*, historically voiced by Hank Azaria, can have an individual storyline, yet the stylized accent and recurring shopkeeper role can make him function as a shorthand for Indianness. In *Master of None*’s “Indians on TV,” Dev’s audition makes the casting demand itself visible and shows how an industry teaches audiences to hear race.

*Reading: Davé, “Racial Accents, Hollywood Casting, and Asian American Studies,” pp. 142–145.*

### 8.7

**Recent shows and films replace accented or culturally specific characters with “race-neutral” or ethnically ambiguous ones. Do you think removing accents and cultural backgrounds makes Hollywood more inclusive—or less honest about difference? How might including authentic accents or multilingual voices change what we consider “American” stories?**

**Sample position.** Open casting expands the jobs available to actors, and an unmarked accent can be appropriate for an Asian American character. But dropping accents and backstories across the board can make white, middle-class U.S. experience the template everyone has to fit. Including specific accents and multilingual dialogue can show that “American” encompasses immigrants and multilingual families. For example, a character could shift languages between home and work while remaining the person whose point of view guides the scene. The meaningful distinction is whether the voice belongs to a developed character or serves primarily as a punch line or a sign that they do not belong.

*Reading: Davé, pp. 143–147, especially colorblind casting and accent neutralization.*

## Week 9: Independent Films and Public Service Media; Bruce Lee: Anti-Imperialism and Local/Global Action Star

### 9.1

**According to Tajima-Peña, why is it insufficient to counter negative stereotypes of Asian Americans with positive representations alone? How does Asian American independent filmmaking’s connection to community activism shape its efforts to challenge dominant cultural narratives?**

Positive images can counter an insult, but an upright spokesperson or success story can still ask Asian Americans to win approval by being exemplary. Tajima-Peña connects image-making to **who controls production and whom it serves**. Early filmmakers worked with organizers and scholars in movements for ethnic studies, civil rights, and against war. This gave their work historical subjects, political audiences, and community distribution routes. A screening could help organize an audience as well as change what it saw. Her account also warns that getting access to larger institutions can alter filmmakers’ priorities if access becomes the whole goal.

*Reading: Tajima-Peña, “Defining a Framework for Asian American Cinema,” “How a Hundred Flowers Bloomed,” and “Entitlement.”*

### 9.2

**According to Tajima-Pena, how can Asian American filmmakers overcome industry barriers?**

Tajima-Peña describes a two-part response. **Build independent capacity** through collectives, skill sharing, professional training, and community screening networks. **Contest established gates** by demanding grants, public TV carriage, access to film schools, festivals, theater screens, and library collections. Visual Communications toured *Hito Hata: Raise the Banner* with local partner groups, building a national audience through sponsored screenings. NAATA helped filmmakers pursue public broadcasting access. The trade-off is that fundraising or broadcast requirements can narrow the work, so access matters most when it gives makers real creative and economic control.

*Reading: Tajima-Peña, “Entitlement” and “The 1980s: Quality Time.”*

### 9.3

**According to Tajima-Pena, what role do independent films play in Asian American representation?**

Independent films can **recover histories, develop new ways of telling them, and create publics**. Filmmakers can show working-class and community life that studio productions often ignore, speak from local perspectives, and try documentary, animation, or experimental forms. Films can also circulate through coalition screenings and public television, enabling audiences to discuss a problem and organize around it. *Who Killed Vincent Chin?* brings an Asian American civil rights case into public view and examines how racism can operate through employment anxiety, news narratives, and legal institutions. Community roots do not require a single approved aesthetic.

*Reading and film: Tajima-Peña, “How a Hundred Flowers Bloomed” and “Entitlement”; Who Killed Vincent Chin?*

### 9.4

**How did the economic conditions in Detroit during the early 1980s contribute to the racial tensions that culminated in Vincent Chin's murder?**

In 1982, Detroit’s auto industry was facing recession, layoffs, and increased competition from Japanese manufacturers. The documentary places the killing alongside appeals to buy American and anger about job losses. Ronald Ebens and Michael Nitz had ties to the auto industry, and witnesses described blame directed at Vincent Chin as if he were responsible for those losses. Chin was Chinese American. Treating an Asian-looking person as the embodiment of Japan shows **racial scapegoating** and the perpetual-foreigner stereotype. Economic insecurity helps explain the setting in which that blame circulated. It does not excuse the attack or prove what every individual was thinking.

*Film evidence: [Who Killed Vincent Chin? (PBS POV transcript)](https://www.pbs.org/video/who-killed-vincent-chin-hhao4q/). Reading connection: Ono and Pham, ch. 5, economic Yellow Peril.*

### 9.5

**How does Who Killed Vincent Chin? reveal the limits of the legal system in addressing anti-Asian racism? How does the documentary portray the potential of collective organizing and the tensions between seeking legal redress and pursuing broader racial justice?**

In state court, the manslaughter proceedings ended in probation and financial penalties, with no prison term. A judge’s emphasis on the defendants’ backgrounds shows how officials can give greater weight to who they believe the defendants are than to the broader racial harm. Organizers including Lily Chin and American Citizens for Justice pushed for federal civil rights prosecution. Ebens was convicted in the first federal trial, that conviction was overturned, and a retrial ended in acquittal. Nitz was acquitted in the initial federal trial. The federal proceeding turned partly on proof of motive, which is harder to establish than the fact of a killing. The case shows the value of organizing for a hearing and public recognition, and the limits of expecting a conviction to provide all the justice a community seeks.

*Film evidence: [Who Killed Vincent Chin? (PBS POV transcript)](https://www.pbs.org/video/who-killed-vincent-chin-hhao4q/).*

### 9.6

**How did the documentary impact your understanding of racial tensions and the judicial system in America?**

**Sample reflection.** The film made me think about how institutions can acknowledge an act of violence while minimizing what makes it publicly significant. The contrast between testimony about anti-Asian blame and the judge’s focus on the defendants’ stable backgrounds shows how a decision can seem routine in court and deeply unequal to the people affected. I also learned that access to civil rights law had to be fought for. American Citizens for Justice helped make anti-Asian violence a public issue even though it could not secure a lasting conviction. The documentary’s interviews make disagreement about what counted as racism part of its evidence.

*Film evidence: Who Killed Vincent Chin? Use a specific moment from your own viewing when adapting this response.*

### 9.7

**Why does Prashad favor polyculturalism over multiculturalism? Does recognizing interconnected cultural histories provide a stronger basis for challenging racism? Support your argument with reasons.**

Prashad’s **polyculturalism** treats cultures as historically linked through travel, trade, migration, coercion, struggle, and mutual borrowing. The version of multiculturalism he critiques imagines bounded cultural groups that should respect or tolerate one another while remaining separate. **Sample argument:** Recognizing shared histories can make solidarity stronger because it shows how groups have already shaped one another and can organize around related structures. Black martial artists’ engagement with Asian fighting forms is an example. The analysis must still name slavery, colonialism, or unequal appropriation where relevant. Cultural exchange can occur under unequal conditions.

*Reading: Prashad, pp. 52–54, 78–81.*

### 9.8

**According to Prasad, where did martial arts originate?**

Prashad provides **competing accounts** instead of awarding martial arts a single birthplace. Kilindi Iyi argues for African origins and points to ancient Egyptian imagery. Another familiar account credits Bodhidharma with bringing South Indian martial practices to China’s Shaolin Temple. Prashad also considers parallels with capoeira Angola, developed by enslaved Africans and their descendants in Brazil. He leaves room for both cultural diffusion and independent invention. For the exam, distinguish the **South India-to-Shaolin origin legend** from **Prashad’s own preference for a polycultural history**.

*Reading: Prashad, pp. 78–80.*

### 9.9

**According to Prasad, why do minority groups turn to their roots and authenticity?**

Groups subjected to racism may claim roots to rebuild dignity, give children a history they can value, demand resources or recognition, and protect cultural practices from appropriation. Prashad sees why these defenses make sense under pressure. He is wary when they turn into claims of racial purity or exclusive ownership. Those claims can hide the actual exchanges that developed a tradition and undermine alliances. A community can defend its labor, heritage, and credit while acknowledging other communities’ contributions.

*Reading: Prashad, pp. 79–81.*

### 9.10

**According to Vijay Prashad, how does Bruce Lee’s political significance extend beyond challenging stereotypes of Asian men to fostering Black–Asian solidarity against racism and imperialism?**

Lee provided an image of Asian agency and also gave audiences across racial and national lines a vocabulary of resistance. Prashad places kung fu fandom in the context of Black freedom movements, anti-imperialist politics, and exchanges among martial artists. Lee’s teaching and training crossed racial boundaries, and Black practitioners and performers transformed what they took from kung fu. For example, Sugar Ray Leonard describes learning from Lee’s movement, and *The Last Dragon* reimagines a Lee-inspired hero in Harlem. The political significance lies partly in what audiences and collaborators did with Lee’s work, even where the plot was commercially constrained or stereotyped.

*Reading: Prashad, pp. 51–55 and 74–81.*

## Week 10: Bruce Lee: Anti-Imperialism & Local/Global Action Star; Mixed Race and Media Matters

### 10.1

**In what ways does Enter the Dragon forge Afro-Asian solidarity against racism and imperialism?**

**Afro-Asian solidarity is strongest in the film’s casting, action, and reception.** Lee is the Chinese hero, and Jim Kelly’s Williams is a skilled Black martial artist with a history and a connection to Black martial arts culture. When white police harass Williams before his departure, his ability to fight back makes kung fu legible as resistance to U.S. racism as well as an Asian tradition. The tournament sets them against the same criminal regime and puts both men on equal footing as fighters. There are limits: Han kills Williams, and Lee and Roper carry the final fight. Lee also takes a British intelligence assignment, so the film’s plot is less consistently anti-imperialist than the reading that Black and other international audiences could make of it.

*Reading and film: Prashad, pp. 74–78; Enter the Dragon (1973), the police encounter, Han’s compound, and the final uprising. [Library of Congress film record](https://www.loc.gov/item/96506175/).*

### 10.2

**How does Enter the Dragon challenge dominant representations of Asian masculinity, and to what extent does it reproduce racial and gender stereotypes?**

Lee’s character is the story’s center of action and judgment. His skill, self-possession, and tactical intelligence overturn familiar portrayals of Asian men as ineffectual sidekicks or merely comic. Yet the film still gives Lee little romantic subjectivity and can make fighting ability the price of being a credible Asian lead. Han mobilizes older images of the sinister Asian criminal mastermind. Williams is confident and capable, but the film also uses the trope of Black male sexual prowess and removes him before the climax. Women are often framed as sexual spectacle or victims, although Mei Ling acts as an undercover ally. The film changes who can be a star while retaining several conventions of its genre and era.

*Reading and film: Ono and Pham, ch. 4, section on Bruce Lee, PDF pp. 13–15; Prashad, pp. 76–78; Enter the Dragon.*

### 10.3

**Who is Han? What does he represent?**

**Han** is a renegade former Shaolin student who uses an island martial arts tournament to conceal a criminal enterprise involving opium and sexual exploitation. He is Lee’s foil. Both have martial training, but Han uses it to accumulate wealth and control others, while Lee uses discipline and skill to defeat him. Han’s compound and elaborate weaponized prosthesis also invoke the exotic, sadistic mastermind associated with Yellow Peril stories. His villainy is one strand of the film. Lee’s heroism creates a competing representation of Chinese identity and martial ability.

*Reading and film: Ono and Pham, chs. 2 and 4; [Enter the Dragon, Library of Congress](https://www.loc.gov/item/96506175/).*

### 10.4

**While Asian American scholar Frank Chin, in Slanted Screen, argues that Bruce Lee is another stereotype of Asian American men, for many Asian Americans Bruce Lee is a role model for empowering themselves as well as challenging the existing negative stereotypes of Asian Americans. Discuss the ways in which Bruce Lee in Enter the Dragon challenges or perpetuates stereotypes of Asian Americans?**

Chin’s objection identifies a limit: if Hollywood will cast an Asian man as a lead only when he is an exceptional martial artist, the film still narrows what Asian men can be. And a body celebrated for its fighting can remain unavailable as a romantic lead. The empowering reading identifies a different fact: Lee is no subordinate foil. He drives the plot, defeats Han, and offers audiences a commanding Asian protagonist at a time when those roles were uncommon. Prashad helps explain how Black and Asian audiences could use that image for purposes beyond the studio’s intentions. A strong answer holds together the conditions of the role and what viewers make of it.

*Reading and film: The Slanted Screen; Ono and Pham, ch. 4, PDF pp. 13–15; Prashad, pp. 51–55, 74–78; Enter the Dragon.*

### 10.5

**How were mixed-race characters typically portrayed in early Hollywood films?**

Beltrán and Fojas identify **sympathetic but doomed figures** and **treacherous threats** as recurring early patterns. A melodrama might center a late revelation of someone’s ancestry and make it destroy an otherwise possible future. The authors discuss *Imitation of Life* and *Pinky* in connection with the “tragic mulatta” convention, which made social exclusion seem an inevitable result of ancestry. D. W. Griffith’s *The Birth of a Nation* depicts the mixed-race character Silas Lynch as dangerous, helping justify white supremacist violence. Across these patterns, mixedness becomes a problem for maintaining a racial boundary, while the character’s life and choices are subordinated to that boundary.

*Reading: Beltrán and Fojas, introduction, pp. 7–11.*

### 10.6

**What role did the Production Code play in the portrayal of mixed-race relationships in Hollywood?**

The industry’s Production Code, enforced from 1934, made interracial intimacy a censorship issue. The express miscegenation provision specified sexual relationships between Black and white people. Studio fears and case-by-case enforcement also shaped other interracial pairings. Some films got through by avoiding consummation, punishing or separating lovers, or using casting that obscured racial difference. Beltrán and Fojas stress that interracial stories did reach the screen, but positive endings, marriage, and children were often restricted. Keep the **formal Code rule** distinct from its **broader industrial effects**.

*Reading: Beltrán and Fojas, pp. 7–9.*

### 10.7

**How have changing social norms and legal decisions, such as the Supreme Court's ruling in Loving v. Virginia, influenced the portrayal of mixed race relationships in U.S. film and television?**

The weakening of the Production Code and changing norms made interracial relationships easier to show. In 1967, *Loving v. Virginia* invalidated state bans on interracial marriage, undermining a legal basis for the taboo. The trend was already underway: in *Sayonara* (1957), one couple resolves to marry despite military opposition, while another couple’s story ends tragically. Later stories make room for lasting relationships, uncertain or hopeful outcomes, and conflicts shaped by class, immigration, or nationality. *Mississippi Masala* develops the couple’s choices in that wider context. Treat the ruling and changes in film practice as related developments with different timelines.

*Reading: Beltrán and Fojas, pp. 6–10. [National Archives case records](https://www.archives.gov/philadelphia/highlights/loving-v-virginia).*

### 10.8

**According to “Introduction: Mixed Race in Hollywood Film and Media Culture” by Beltrán and Fojas, what has been one of the impacts of the increased visibility of mixed-race actors in mainstream media?**

One impact is that **mixed-race people can become publicly recognizable as stars and protagonists**, which challenges the older assumption that a performer must fit a single fixed racial category. The authors cite actors such as Vin Diesel and Dwayne Johnson in a discussion of the rise of mixed-race action stars. Beltrán and Fojas also raise a question about that visibility: an industry can market a racially ambiguous look while leaving a character’s heritage unspoken. A performer’s ancestry, the character’s racial identity, and what audiences perceive can differ. Increased visibility changes who can appear and leaves open how and on whose terms they appear.

*Reading: Beltrán and Fojas, pp. 1–7, 9–13.*

### 10.9

**What are the primary reasons (historical backdrop) for Indian community’s displacement from Uganda, as shown in Mira Nair’s Mississippi Masala?**

The immediate cause was **Idi Amin’s 1972 expulsion order** against Uganda’s Asian population. The longer history includes British colonial rule, during which South Asians came to East Africa as workers, traders, and professionals. Colonial institutions helped establish racialized economic divisions. Post-independence resentment over perceived economic privilege and calls for Africanization provided a context for Amin’s scapegoating and seizure or redistribution of Asian-owned property. Jay’s family shows why the label “outsider” was politically imposed: many of those displaced were born in Uganda and understood it as home.

*Film evidence: [Mississippi Masala (Criterion)](https://www.criterion.com/films/32237-mississippi-masala) and [AFI synopsis](https://catalog.afi.com/Film/59326-MISSISSIPPI-MASALA). Reading connection: Beltrán and Fojas, p. 9, on migration and nationality in interracial narratives.*

### 10.10

**What does Jay’s continued legal battle for his Ugandan property symbolize?**

For Jay, the property case symbolizes **recognition that the expulsion was a wrong and that he belonged in Uganda**. A house is an asset, but it also stands for work, memory, friendship, and the life his family expected to continue. A court hearing allows him to imagine that the state could recognize a claim it once erased. His eventual return confronts him with a limit of that hope: restitution could address a material loss, but it could not restore his friendship with Okelo or recreate the community he left.

*Film evidence: Mississippi Masala, Jay’s letters and restitution case, final return to Kampala.*

### 10.11

**How does Mississippi Masala explore the concept of home?**

The film gives home several meanings. Jay ties it to Uganda, where he grew up and built his life. Kinnu’s work and long years in exile show what it takes to sustain a household in a new place. Mina was born in Uganda, lived in England, and lives in Mississippi without ever having visited India. Her description of herself as a “masala” expresses a layered sense of belonging. By the end, Jay tells Kinnu that his home is with her in Mississippi, while Mina and Demetrius leave together toward a future elsewhere. Home can be inherited, forcibly lost, built through care, and chosen.

*Film evidence: [Mississippi Masala (AFI synopsis)](https://catalog.afi.com/Film/59326-MISSISSIPPI-MASALA), especially the birthday-party conversation and final phone calls.*

### 10.12

**How does Mississippi Masala's portray cross-cultural relationships and tensions between the Indian and African American communities?**

Mina and Demetrius’s relationship grows through shared humor, attraction, and ordinary encounters. It also exposes Indian community policing of marriage and reputation, anti-Black prejudice and colorism, and the racial and economic pressures Demetrius faces in Mississippi. The motel owners’ withdrawal of carpet-cleaning work after the Biloxi incident shows that prejudice has material consequences. Earlier, an owner invokes solidarity while trying to discourage a potential lawsuit after the car accident. Demetrius later appeals to their shared racialization in a conversation with Jay. The film distinguishes genuine solidarity from the selective use of shared identity when business interests are at stake.

*Film evidence: Mississippi Masala, the car accident, motel boycott, and Jay–Demetrius conversation. Reading connection: Beltrán and Fojas, p. 9.*

### 10.13

**How do the experiences of displacement and racial prejudice depicted in Mississippi Masala resonate with contemporary examples of cross-cultural solidarity (or lack thereof)?**

**Sample comparison.** In the film, experiencing expulsion does not guarantee that Jay will recognize Demetrius’s experience of anti-Black racism. Conversely, Demetrius’s appeal to shared marginalization does not erase their different histories. The 2020 *Letters for Black Lives* project provides a parallel example of how solidarity can be made through concrete, multilingual conversations about anti-Blackness within Asian diasporic families. It contrasts with the motel owners’ brief claim of solidarity followed by a boycott. Shared vulnerability can provide a starting point for a coalition. It takes sustained action, and a willingness to name unequal power within and across communities, to make that coalition work.

*Film and contemporary example: Mississippi Masala; [Letters for Black Lives translators’ accounts](https://aaww.org/notes-from-translators-letters-for-black-lives/). Reading connection: Prashad, pp. 52–54, on solidarity across connected histories.*

### 10.14

**Why does Okello says “Africa for Africans, black Africans” to Jay, his childhood friend?**

Okelo (spelled “Okello” in the handout) says this after Jay insists Uganda is his home. The remark voices the racialized definition of belonging ascendant during Amin’s expulsion and conveys that Jay’s birthplace and years there no longer protect him. It also follows Jay’s public criticism of a dangerous regime, and Okelo urges him to leave. The film allows the line to carry both injury and a warning. Jay hears it as betrayal after years of friendship. Mina remembers that Okelo risked his life for Jay, and Jay later learns the secret police killed Okelo. These scenes give us reason to question Jay’s assumption that the words captured the whole relationship.

*Film evidence: [Mississippi Masala (AFI synopsis)](https://catalog.afi.com/Film/59326-MISSISSIPPI-MASALA), the expulsion, flashback, and return sequences.*

### 10.15

**In what ways does Jay’s fight for his Ugandan property in Mississippi Masala reflect the emotional and political challenges faced by displaced communities seeking to reclaim their lost homes?**

The legal claim shows **two kinds of repair**. Material repair would address dispossession and the loss of family wealth. Political repair would require a government to acknowledge that it wronged people it had excluded from the nation. There is also grief that a legal decision cannot resolve. Jay returns after years of delay and discovers that Okelo is dead, so a ruling could never give him back the Uganda he remembers. For displaced people, return, restitution, safety, and a felt sense of belonging can come apart. The film makes this difference visible by following the lawsuit and Jay’s personal reckoning together.

*Film evidence: Mississippi Masala, the delayed court hearing and Jay’s return. Compare the distinction between legal remedies and collective justice in Who Killed Vincent Chin?*

## Week 11: Framing, War on Terror, and Islamophobia

### 11.1

**Why do Mina’s parents disapprove of Mina’s relationship with Demetrius even though they were born and raised in Uganda? To what extent does Mississippi Masala illustrate key shifts from negative stereotypes to more positive portrayals of mixed-race identities and relationships, as described in "Mixed Race Hollywood" by Beltrán and Fojas?**

Growing up in Uganda does not automatically undo **anti-Blackness, colorism, or pressure to marry within the community**. Kinnu worries that Mina’s dark complexion and independence will hurt her marriage prospects. After the motel incident, both parents are concerned with family reputation and community ties. Jay also projects his hurt over Uganda onto Mina’s relationship. He expects racial boundaries to reassert themselves and thinks he is protecting her from the rejection he associates with Okelo. Mina challenges that reading by remembering Okelo’s care for Jay.

The film illustrates Beltrán and Fojas’s shift because the romance is emotionally serious and given a possible future. Mina and Demetrius get to confront their own choices and decide to leave together. The film still depicts economic retaliation, prejudice, and family tension. The change lies in whose lives and choices drive the story, and in its escape from the convention that crossing a racial boundary must end in tragedy. Mina’s “masala” describes a mixed cultural and diasporic identity. The film need not establish that either lover is of mixed racial ancestry to count as a portrayal of an interracial relationship.

*Reading and film: Beltrán and Fojas, pp. 7–10; [Mississippi Masala (AFI synopsis)](https://catalog.afi.com/Film/59326-MISSISSIPPI-MASALA).*

### 11.2

**How do Reese and Lewis conceptualize the role of media in framing the War on Terror, and what specific examples do they provide to illustrate this framing?**

For Reese and Lewis, a **frame** is a shared organizing principle that makes some connections seem natural and others less visible. Bush officials sponsored the War on Terror frame after 9/11, and journalism helped circulate and elaborate it. The authors distinguish three levels using *USA Today* examples.

| Level | What the press does | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Transmission | Relays the administration’s label as shorthand for specified policies. | Reporting that Bush made the war on terrorism his administration’s central focus. |
| Reification | Treats the abstract policy as a concrete event or an accepted national undertaking. | A recurring headline guide grouping the day’s developments under the war against terrorism. |
| Naturalization | Treats the policy frame as the common-sense reality through which otherwise unrelated events make sense. | A *USA Today* editorial borrows Yogi Berra’s baseball idiom to frame the war as an ongoing contest that everyone is already in. |

These are interpretive distinctions. They identify different uses of the frame, not three stages every journalist passed through in order.

*Reading: Reese and Lewis, pp. 777–779, 783–789.*

### 11.3

**According to Reese and Lewis, what are the primary media strategies that were used to shape public perception of the War on Terror, and how did these strategies impact public opinion?**

The key strategies were **repeated use of the official label**, **less attribution to the administration**, and **embedding it in familiar narratives of national unity, danger, and victory**. Routine reporting relied on official statements, and headings and editorials grouped varied events under the same frame. This made policies such as military action seem part of one defensive project instead of separate choices needing separate justification. It also made Iraq easier to associate with the post-9/11 threat without proof that Iraq carried out the attacks. The study primarily analyzes news texts and interviews. It explains a mechanism shaping public understanding, but it does not isolate a causal effect on every audience member’s opinion.

*Reading: Reese and Lewis, pp. 783–789, 792–793.*

### 11.4

**According to Reese and Lewis, what role did the media play in legitimizing the Bush administration's policies post-9/11?**

The press helped **legitimize** administration policy when it treated the War on Terror as a national undertaking instead of a contestable Bush administration choice. It often left readers debating tactics, competence, and whether the country was winning, with less attention to the open-ended war framework or the rationale for grouping Iraq and Afghanistan together. Reliance on official sources and the patriotic atmosphere after 9/11 supported this process. Some journalists resisted, and criticism grew as the Iraq war undermined the administration’s credibility. Reese and Lewis argue that scrutiny mattered most before the major policy decisions had been made.

*Reading: Reese and Lewis, pp. 778–779, 789–793.*

### 11.5

**Why is there so much support for policies (such as the Patriot Act II and II) that are apparently anathema to basic American values?**

Fear of another attack and the language of emergency made expanded state power seem necessary. The War on Terror frame presented surveillance and detention as protective measures, and opposition could be made to look like a failure of patriotism. The costs were distributed unequally: many people could imagine greater protection for themselves while seeing little of the consequences for Arab, Muslim, South Asian, or immigrant communities. Alsultany shows how official anti-discrimination language and images of patriotic Muslims could support the idea that only dangerous people would be targeted. That appearance made structural discrimination easier to overlook.

The handout repeats “II.” Distinguish the **USA PATRIOT Act enacted in 2001** from **“PATRIOT Act II,” the proposed 2003 Domestic Security Enhancement Act**. The latter was a draft and did not become law as a package. Explain support for the broader security framework without assuming the two documents had the same legislative status or level of public support.

*Reading: Alsultany, introduction, pp. 4–7, 12–15; Reese and Lewis, pp. 777–779, 788–793. [Enacted statute](https://www.congress.gov/107/statute/STATUTE-115/STATUTE-115-Pg272.pdf) and [2003 draft text](https://w2.eff.org/Censorship/Terrorism_militias/patriot2draft.html).*

### 11.6

**How do Reese and Lewis explain the U.S. press’s role in transforming the “War on Terror” from a government policy frame into a taken-for-granted way of understanding the world? How did this framing help legitimize military action and limit public debate?**

An attributed phrase such as “Bush’s War on Terror” still leaves room to ask who defined it and why. When headlines and stories routinely refer to “the war” as an existing reality, they turn a political decision into a fact of life. When the same frame organizes elections, international alliances, or even sports metaphors, it becomes a shared way to interpret the world. The question shifts from “Should the U.S. pursue this policy?” to “How should the U.S. win this war?” That shift helped make the Iraq invasion appear to follow from 9/11 and pushed alternative responses, relevant differences among conflicts, and the scope of civil liberties into the background.

*Reading: Reese and Lewis, pp. 778–779, 784–789, 792–793.*

### 11.7

**How does Evelyn Alsultany define the concept of "simplified complex representations" in post-9/11 media narratives?**

**Simplified complex representations** are devices that make a story look nuanced about race while keeping the larger security story in place. The signature example is placing a sympathetic Arab or Muslim American patriot, government agent, or hate-crime victim beside an Arab or Muslim terrorist. The sympathetic character complicates the idea that every member of the group is dangerous. Yet the story may still divide characters into “good” and “bad” according to loyalty to the United States and make terrorism the main context in which Arabs and Muslims appear. The issue is the work the arrangement does for the story as a whole.

*Reading: Alsultany, introduction, pp. 13–16.*

### 11.8

**According to Alsultany, how did the U.S. media's portrayal of Arabs and Muslims change after September 11, 2001?**

Alsultany identifies a **rise in sympathetic and patriotic portrayals after 9/11**, alongside continued and often prominent terrorism narratives. Before 9/11, Hollywood often relied on a generic Arab enemy, exotic settings, or one-dimensional terrorist roles. After 9/11, prime-time dramas such as *24* were more likely to balance an alleged enemy with a loyal agent, a neighbor, or a victim of racist attack. This was a shift in the conventions of representation, even as profiling and other forms of institutional discrimination intensified. Keep “Arab” and “Muslim” distinct: one is an ethnolinguistic identity and the other is a religious identity, with some overlap. U.S. media often conflate them, and anti-Muslim racialization can affect people perceived as Muslim regardless of their faith.

*Reading: Alsultany, introduction, pp. 1–6, 7–12, 14–16.*

### 11.9

**How does Evelyn Alsultany's concept of "simplified complex representations" challenge the assumption that positive portrayals of Arabs and Muslims necessarily counter racism? In what ways can sympathetic representations coexist with, or even support, policies and practices associated with the War on Terror?**

A sympathetic character can show how unfair everyday prejudice is while leaving the audience to accept a system of surveillance, detention, or exceptional state force as necessary. For example, *24* includes Nadia Yassir, an Arab American counterterrorism agent. A loyal agent can challenge a stereotype about individual loyalty and simultaneously reassure viewers that the security apparatus is inclusive and can identify the “real” enemy. A story about a hate-crime victim can locate racism in one prejudiced individual, leaving discriminatory institutions outside the frame. Alsultany calls attention to this **post-race illusion**. Ask whether sympathetic characters can belong without proving loyalty and whether the story challenges the policy as well as the prejudice.

*Reading: Alsultany, introduction, pp. 10, 11–17.*

### 11.10

**Evelyn Alsultany, in “Introduction,” Arabs and Muslims in the Media: Race and Representation after 9/11,” state that the media portrayal of the War on Terror and the U.S. government’s portrayal of the recent history as interrelated. Similarly, Stephen D. Reese and Seth C. Lewis, in “Framing the War on Terror: The internalization of policy in the US press,” state that “the War on Terror became a socially shared organizing principle through its transmission via the US press” (p. 778). Discuss the ways in which narratives about “global war on terror” are framed, highlighting the role of media in shaping public perceptions and policies towards Arabs and Muslims.**

**Model essay answer.** Reese and Lewis show how state policy became journalism’s common language. Bush officials framed 9/11 as the opening of a global war, and the press transmitted the label, reified it as a shared undertaking, and naturalized it through familiar stories about patriotism and victory. This process made different conflicts appear to be part of one struggle and helped make Iraq seem a logical response to 9/11. Debating how to win within that frame left less room to debate whether a war was the right response.

Alsultany shows how entertainment made that framework emotionally plausible. Post-9/11 dramas might pair a Muslim terrorist with a loyal Muslim agent or a sympathetic victim of hate. Viewers could reject explicit anti-Muslim prejudice and still accept the recurring image of a hidden security threat. This structure echoes the combination of official tolerance and practices such as surveillance, special registration, and detention discussed in the reading. Taken together, the readings explain how consent can form through a common language and through stories that make some uses of power seem responsible. Reese and Lewis study how the frame circulates in news. Alsultany studies how apparent inclusion can coexist with racialized exclusion.

*Reading: Reese and Lewis, pp. 778–779, 784–793; Alsultany, introduction, pp. 3–7, 11–17.*

## Week 12: Contesting Representations: New APIA Representations in Mainstream and Independent Media

### 12.1

**How does Eve Oishi define the concept of “bad Asian”? Do you think that the label of “bad Asian” can somehow be beneficial for Asians in challenging the longstanding stereotype of Asian Americans as the model minority, despite its negative origins? In other words, should Asian Americans use the “bad Asian” label as a strategy to challenge the model minority stereotype?**

Oishi uses **“Bad Asian”** playfully, as in “badass,” for people who depart from roles assigned by white society or resist racism, sexism, class oppression, or homophobia. The pool hall at the start of the chapter offers an example: young Asian Americans are loud and openly enjoying themselves in ways that clash with the stereotype of quiet respectability. Oishi also notes that people may be outside the boundaries of approval because they have already faced poverty, segregation, or harassment.

**Sample position.** Reclaiming “Bad Asian” can reveal that the model minority is a demand for behavior rather than a description of a group. The label works best as a voluntary, context-specific strategy. Imposing it on other people could reproduce the very judgment it is meant to unsettle. Asian Americans should also have room for different choices about when to refuse, negotiate, or ignore respectability.

*Reading: Oishi, pp. 221–223.*

### 12.2

**How does Eve Oishi’s concept of “Bad Asians” challenge expectations about what Asian American filmmakers should represent? How does queer Asian American artists’ playful engagement with popular culture constitute a political intervention even when their work does not explicitly foreground racial identity?**

Earlier independent filmmakers and queer artists of color created access, told histories that had been omitted, and directly addressed distorted or absent representations. Oishi argues that later artists inherit those gains without having to make racial identity the explicit subject of every work. A film can be personal, funny, erotic, or formally experimental without speaking for a whole community or supplying a lesson about identity.

In *Love Song for Persis K.*, David Dasharath Kalal reframes film clips of Persis Khambatta through color and music. Oishi reads this fandom as a way for a queer South Asian spectator to make an image marked as “alien” into a resource for identification. In *Hindustan*, Gita Reddy and Kalal give the Crosby and Clooney vocal tracks to unexpected performers through lip-sync and end with same-sex couples dancing and kissing. The rearrangement exposes the exoticizing and heterosexual assumptions of the song and creates space for queer South Asian desire. **The form of the work changes what familiar culture can mean and who can claim it.**

*Reading: Oishi, pp. 222–228, 231–232, 238.*

### 12.3

**Oishi contrasts the “Good Asian” with the “Bad Asian.” What does this contrast show about racial respectability in U.S. culture? In your answer, explain why Oishi treats “badness” not simply as deviance, but as a political challenge to dominant expectations of Asian American behavior.**

The contrast reveals that **respectability is a condition of approval**. Oishi’s “Good Asian” is expected to be quiet and supportive of the status quo, as in her example of a *Star Trek* officer ready to sacrifice himself rather than leave his white colleague. She calls the activist Yuri Kochiyama a “Bad Asian” because Kochiyama’s public militancy and multiracial alliances defy the fantasy that Asian Americans will avoid confrontation. What dominant culture treats as bad behavior can make inequality visible and challenge who gets to define proper conduct. The examples also show why loudness alone is an incomplete definition. A person can find themselves outside respectability whether or not they intended a political gesture.

*Reading: Oishi, pp. 221–222, 224.*

### 12.4

**How might queer cinema reveal, complicate, or challenge the hypersexualization of Asian women and the emasculation or desexualization of Asian men?**

Queer cinema can ask **who has desire, who is desired, and whose point of view controls an image**. Ono and Pham trace how Asian women are made available as racialized objects while Asian men are often denied romantic or sexual agency. Oishi’s reading of *Paradice* shows how two performers restage an apparently heterosexual transaction and expose the fantasies that tie racialized sexuality to tourism and consumption. In Nguyen Tan Hoang’s *Maybe Never (But I’m Counting the Days)*, young Asian Americans appear as people who desire, are desired, and make fantasies. Its staging also unsettles the idea that filmed testimony is simply unmediated truth. These works make room for agency and complexity without requiring that every Asian woman be sexually restrained or that every Asian man prove his masculinity through heterosexual conquest.

*Reading: Ono and Pham, ch. 4, PDF pp. 1–8; Oishi, pp. 228–229, 233–235; compare Shimizu, The Hypersexuality of Race, on agency within racialized sexual images.*

### 12.5

**How might recent legislative and political developments constrain the creation of queer Asian American media? To what extent might these pressures produce more assimilative or “Good Asian” forms of representation from major media companies or artists?**

**Application of Oishi to later developments.** Florida’s 2022 HB 1557 restricted certain K–12 classroom instruction on sexual orientation and gender identity. A January 2025 federal executive order also directed agencies to plan how to address K–12 education funding linked to instruction it described as “gender ideology.” These are examples of political pressure on educational access and funding, distinct from a prohibition on making or privately releasing a film. The effects could reach filmmakers indirectly if schools stop booking screenings, funders avoid controversial work, or distributors decide a project is too risky. Which effects occur depends on the policy, how it is implemented, and the institution.

For a major studio, this may encourage a “Good Asian” version of inclusion: a successful, respectable character whose queerness is brief, easily edited, or detached from desires and community life. Independent creators may also feel funding pressure, though festivals, community screenings, and small distributors can give them other routes to audiences. Oishi helps explain the pressure toward acceptable visibility and why access to production and distribution matters. She did not write about these later policies.

*Reading: Oishi, pp. 221–225, 237–239. Policy examples: [Florida Senate bill summary](https://www.flsenate.gov/Committees/billsummaries/2022/html/1557) and [January 2025 executive order](https://www.whitehouse.gov/presidential-actions/2025/01/ending-radical-indoctrination-in-k-12-schooling/).*

### 12.6

**What do you think are the next steps or challenges for achieving even more nuanced representations of Asian Americans in media?**

**Sample position.** The next step is to give more people the power to decide what stories get made and circulated. Fund independent creators, sustain public and community screening spaces, and hire Asian Americans as writers, directors, producers, and decision-makers as well as performers. Build a range of roles across class, ethnicity, generation, sexuality, and gender. Use open casting where background is incidental and culturally specific writing where it matters. A nuanced Asian American character should have relationships, flaws, desires, and a point of view without carrying the burden of representing everyone. The challenge is to broaden audience access while protecting the kinds of artistic risk Oishi values.

*Reading: Oishi, pp. 222–225, 237–239; Tajima-Peña, “Entitlement”; Davé, pp. 143–147.*
