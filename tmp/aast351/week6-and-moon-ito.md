## Week 6

### 1. How was the “Asian mystique” produced through Western imperial encounters?

Prasso traces the mystique to Western contact and storytelling: travel accounts, colonial encounters, and later media repeatedly framed Asia as erotic, exotic, dangerous, and feminine. These descriptions reveal the Western gaze and its power relations, not a shared truth about Asian women. In her interview, Prasso says this lens distorts present-day interactions; the book description says she contrasts stereotypes with portraits of women’s varied lives. [Prasso interview](https://americanarchive.org/catalog/cpb-aacip-16-z892805n9b); [book description](https://books.google.com/books/about/The_Asian_Mystique.html?id=0elZBgAAQBAJ).

### 2. What role did historical accounts and popular culture play? How did the “Dragon Lady” become associated with Asian women?

Travel writing and popular culture repeatedly framed Asia as alluring and perilous, and Asian women as submissive or cunning. The “Dragon Lady” condenses the latter fantasy into a powerful, calculating woman whose sexuality and agency threaten Western men. Prasso’s interview connects early travel writing to contemporary film and television; the book description identifies the Dragon Lady and submissive geisha as recurring Western literary, stage, and screen icons. [Prasso interview](https://americanarchive.org/catalog/cpb-aacip-16-z892805n9b); [book description](https://books.google.com/books/about/The_Asian_Mystique.html?id=0elZBgAAQBAJ).

### 3. How did early European travel writers, like Marco Polo, contribute to Western fantasies?

Travel narratives made distant places legible through marvelous, sensational, and sometimes threatening stories. Prasso’s interview explicitly places Western ideas of Asia’s danger, sensuality, and intrigue as far back as Marco Polo, then follows them into modern entertainment. The point is not that Polo invented every stereotype, but that travel writing helped circulate a “mysterious Orient” frame later reused by writers and filmmakers. [Prasso interview](https://americanarchive.org/catalog/cpb-aacip-16-z892805n9b); [interview excerpt](https://www.proquest.com/trade-journals/not-getting-asia/docview/212093848/se-2).

### 4. How did Victorian-era views on sexuality contrast with Western perceptions of Asian women?

Victorian ideals in respectable Western society emphasized chastity and control of women’s sexuality. Yet Western accounts projected sensuality and sexual availability onto Asian women, treating them as an erotic exception to the rules imposed on white women. That contrast let the West condemn women’s sexual agency at home while consuming fantasies of an uninhibited “Orient.” This answer synthesizes Prasso’s broader argument; her full discussion was not accessible online, so check the assigned chapter for its precise framing.

### 5. How did the portrayal of Empress Dowager Tzu Hsi contribute to the “Dragon Lady” stereotype?

Western accounts cast Tzu Hsi as a ruthless, manipulative ruler and embellished her image with sensational claims about sexual appetite and palace intrigue. Prasso uses the story as an example of Western audiences accepting lurid Orientalist tales as proof of a “Dragon Lady.” The biography by Edmund Backhouse, a source for the legend, was exposed as a hoax in 1974, undermining the supposed evidence for this sexualized portrait. [Accessible Prasso excerpt/review](https://www.thriftbooks.com/w/the-asian-mystique-dragon-ladies-geisha-girls-and-our-fantasies-of-the-exotic-orient_sheridan-prasso/411535/); [Google Books on *Dragon Lady*](https://books.google.com/books/about/Dragon_Lady.html?id=J07_tPJu9M8C).

### 6. How does the “Dragon Lady” stereotype depict Asian American women?

It depicts an Asian woman as cold, calculating, sexually manipulative, and dangerous: she uses beauty or sexuality to gain control instead of appearing as a person with a full inner life. She is framed as the aggressive counterpart to the submissive “Lotus Blossom” or geisha. Both reduce varied women and cultures to Western fantasies. Prasso’s book description identifies the domineering Dragon Lady as a persistent screen and stage icon. [Book description](https://books.google.com/books/about/The_Asian_Mystique.html?id=0elZBgAAQBAJ).

### 7. What is one main reason the “Dragon Lady” image persists?

One reason is that familiar media images are easy to recycle: audiences recognize the trope, and storytellers use it to signal danger, exoticism, or sexual power quickly. Prasso also argues that the wider “Asian mystique” makes Asia seem fundamentally different and easier for Westerners to approach through simplified expectations. Her interview traces these images from early travel writing into contemporary film and television. [Prasso interview](https://americanarchive.org/catalog/cpb-aacip-16-z892805n9b); [book description](https://books.google.com/books/about/The_Asian_Mystique.html?id=0elZBgAAQBAJ).

### 8. What does the “Lotus Blossom” stereotype represent, and how does it contrast with the “Dragon Lady”?

The Lotus Blossom is idealized as delicate, deferential, and sexually available to a Western man, often sacrificing herself for him. The Dragon Lady is framed as her opposite: assertive, controlling, and threatening. Yet both make Asian women serve a Western fantasy—submission in one case, danger in the other. Prasso’s publisher description supports the pairing by contrasting a submissive, sexually available geisha with a steely Dragon Lady. [Book description](https://books.google.com/books/about/The_Asian_Mystique.html?id=0elZBgAAQBAJ).

### 9. What does *Picturing Oriental Girls* tell us about media portrayals of Asian and Asian American women?

Valerie Soe’s 1992 video assembles stereotypical images from American film and television and juxtaposes them with text from mail-order-bride catalogs, men’s magazines, and popular writing. This shows how the geisha, China Doll, and Dragon Lady circulate across entertainment and sexual commerce, making exoticism seem ordinary. The film’s montage invites viewers to notice repetition and question who produced these images. The film itself was not found online; this answer uses catalog and screening descriptions. [WorldCat](https://search.worldcat.org/title/Picturing-oriental-girls-%3A-a-RE-educational-videotape/oclc/28132367); [screening program](https://shapeshifterscinema.com/gravlens/Program-Notes_GL-2.pdf).

## Week 3

### 22. According to Robert B. Ito, how did Hollywood justify casting white actors in yellowface?

Ito identifies recurring defenses: audiences supposedly preferred white performers; Asian actors were said to be unavailable or unqualified; and white actors were claimed to make more convincing “Orientals.” He argues these excuses concealed job protection and a self-reinforcing barrier: Asian performers could not gain experience when major roles went to white actors. In the *Miss Saigon* dispute, Ito says “artistic freedom” and “nontraditional casting” also covered an economic decision. [Ito, “A Certain Slant”](https://brightlightsfilm.com/certain-slant-brief-history-hollywood-yellowface/).

### 23. According to Krystyn Moon, how did nineteenth-century European and American commentators describe Chinese music?

Moon describes Euro-American commentators calling Chinese music “noise” or “un-musical,” and portraying it as lacking qualities Western listeners treated as proper music, especially harmony and refinement. Some accounts called Chinese singing shrill or strange. These were not neutral descriptions: they converted unfamiliar musical systems into supposed evidence of Chinese inferiority. The publisher confirms the book’s focus on racialized musical representation; scholarship citing Moon summarizes the “noise” label and its exclusionary implications. [Rutgers University Press](https://www.bibliovault.org/BV.book.epl?ISBN=9780813535074); [scholarly discussion citing Moon](https://dergipark.org.tr/en/download/article-file/996742).

### 24. How does Moon connect Western views of Chinese music to Orientalism?

Moon links musical judgments to a racial hierarchy: Western commentators treated European music as rational and sophisticated while labeling Chinese music primitive or noisy. This turned cultural difference into an apparent measure of racial capacity and helped justify exclusion from Western performance institutions and anti-Chinese discrimination. The publisher summarizes Moon’s analysis of music’s role in constructing stereotypes; scholarship citing the book directly connects the “noise” label with inferiority and exclusion. [Rutgers University Press](https://www.bibliovault.org/BV.book.epl?ISBN=9780813535074); [scholarly discussion citing Moon](https://dergipark.org.tr/en/download/article-file/996742).
