# AAST351 Study Guide — Week 4 (Q27–35)

**27.** Dhingra and Rodriguez’s intersectional lens shows media placing Asian Americans in racial, gendered, and sexual hierarchies; the guide’s excerpt identifies Asian men as a threat to white women. Ono and Pham explain that repeated images build shared “conceptual maps.” Their Yellow Peril discourse casts Asians as threatening and whites as vulnerable. They call its reproduction across time, institutions, and media “structural embeddedness.” New formats can change the surface while inherited power relations keep old meanings available. [Dhingra & Rodriguez, official book description](https://www.drpawandhingra.com/asian-america); [Ono & Pham, ch. 2, pp. 26–28](https://asame30.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2017/02/ono-and-pham-ch-2.pdf).

**28.** In the climax, Fu Manchu displays captured Sheila Barton, the white heroine, to his followers and asks if they want women like her for wives. After they agree, he orders them to conquer and take white women. Earlier, he recognizes Fah Lo See’s desire for Terry and arranges to drug Terry into becoming her “more-than-willing slave.” Asian men are framed as a collective sexual threat to white women; Asian women are shown coercing white men. White women become prizes and white men their protectors. These examples describe the film’s framing, not real Asian people. [AFI Catalog synopsis](https://catalog.afi.com/Catalog/moviedetails/5880); [screenplay transcript](https://www.springfieldspringfield.co.uk/movie_script.php?movie=the-mask-of-fu-manchu).

**29.** A plausible preferred reading is that Fu Manchu threatens Western civilization and British heroes restore order by recovering relics and rescuing Sheila. His plan to destroy the white race and the finale’s rallying speech encode that opposition. The film cannot establish how most 1930s viewers interpreted it: Hall allows negotiated and oppositional readings, and audience claims need reception evidence. AFI lists contemporary trade-review sources, but representative audience data and enough accessible reviews to establish a majority response were not located. A 1972 Japanese American Citizens League objection documents later opposition, not 1930s opinion. [AFI Catalog history](https://catalog.afi.com/Catalog/moviedetails/5880); [screenplay transcript](https://www.springfieldspringfield.co.uk/movie_script.php?movie=the-mask-of-fu-manchu).

**30.** The film turns a British expedition into a struggle against an imagined Asian invasion. Fu Manchu seeks Genghis Khan’s sword and mask to unite followers, defeat the West, and destroy the white race. Secret headquarters, torture, threatening weapons, snakes, and exoticized ritual make “the Orient” appear mysterious and dangerous. Most clearly, Sheila is exhibited as a sexual reward before an Asian crowd, followed by Fu Manchu’s order to conquer and take white women. Ono and Pham define Yellow Peril as imagining Asians as an invading threat and whites as endangered; the film dramatizes that discourse. [Ono & Pham, ch. 2, pp. 26–28](https://asame30.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2017/02/ono-and-pham-ch-2.pdf); [AFI synopsis](https://catalog.afi.com/Catalog/moviedetails/5880); [transcript](https://www.springfieldspringfield.co.uk/movie_script.php?movie=the-mask-of-fu-manchu).

**31.** Fah Lo See reinforces the Dragon Lady image through eroticized power and cruelty: she desires Terry, orders him whipped, and plans to drug him into sexual compliance. She complicates the passive Asian woman stereotype because she expresses desire and wields authority, but the film codes this agency as sinister and threatening. It ultimately remains subordinated to Fu Manchu’s racial war. The contrast with Sheila, the white woman who must be rescued, frames Asian female sexuality as dangerous to white masculinity and white womanhood as an ideal to protect. This is an analysis of the film’s construction, not Asian women’s lives. [AFI synopsis](https://catalog.afi.com/Catalog/moviedetails/5880); [transcript](https://www.springfieldspringfield.co.uk/movie_script.php?movie=the-mask-of-fu-manchu); [Ono & Pham, pp. 34–35](https://asame30.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2017/02/ono-and-pham-ch-2.pdf).

**32.** Ono and Pham trace Yellow Peril to older Western invasion fears, including stories of Genghis Khan and Mongol expansion; it spread broadly in the late nineteenth century. It fused racial and sexual anxieties with the idea that the East could overwhelm the West. U.S. cartoons, newspapers, and early films depicted Chinese people as alien and unassimilable. The target later broadened across Asian groups and shifted with politics, while the threat to white nationhood persisted. [Ono & Pham, ch. 2, pp. 28–30, 34–37, 42–43](https://asame30.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2017/02/ono-and-pham-ch-2.pdf).

**33.** Yellow Peril’s central pattern is an Asian-white threat binary: Asians appear invasive or culturally threatening while whites seem vulnerable and entitled to defend the nation. Ono and Pham connect this discourse to immigration restrictions, arguing that it helped legitimate laws restricting Chinese migration by casting Chinese immigrants as unassimilable and dangerous. The chapter provides the media-historical connection; consult the statute itself for exact legal provisions. [Ono & Pham, pp. 26–30](https://asame30.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2017/02/ono-and-pham-ch-2.pdf); [National Archives, Chinese Exclusion Act](https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/chinese-exclusion-act).

**34.** I agree that media racial hegemony persists, though audiences and creators contest it. Ono and Pham describe a dominant racial “conceptual map” reproduced across media and time. Their examples range from Fu Manchu to later depictions of Asians as gangsters, economic rivals, or threats linked to globalization. The recurring frames remain available even when the target or medium changes. Hegemony means unequal influence, not total control over interpretation. [Ono & Pham, pp. 26–28, 34–35, 41–44](https://asame30.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2017/02/ono-and-pham-ch-2.pdf).

**35.** I partly agree. Transnational media can build familiarity, but contact alone does not ensure nuanced understanding; works can make other cultures seem strange or exotic. Ono and Pham argue media build shared conceptual maps and unequal production power shapes which images circulate. Global connections can also support translation and self-representation, complicating stereotypes. Globalization can bring people into contact and reproduce distance at once; the outcome depends on who tells the story and what perspectives audiences encounter. [Ono & Pham, pp. 27–28, 42–44](https://asame30.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2017/02/ono-and-pham-ch-2.pdf).
