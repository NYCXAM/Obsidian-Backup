# AAST 351 — Readings and Films Summary

## Week 1

### Edward Said — *Orientalism*, Introduction

Said begins by explaining that the “Orient” is not simply a neutral geographic name for places in Asia or the Middle East. Western societies have spent centuries producing ideas, stories, scholarship, and images about the Orient. These representations often portray the East as fundamentally different from the West. Said is careful, though, not to claim that actual Eastern societies are imaginary. Real peoples, cultures, histories, and nations obviously exist; his concern is with the **Western meanings that get placed onto them**.

He then explains several meanings of **Orientalism**. It can refer to the academic study of the Orient, but it also means a broader way of thinking that divides the world into **Orient versus Occident**, or East versus West. Most importantly, Said describes Orientalism as an entire Western system for dealing with the Orient: Western institutions describe it, study it, make claims about it, teach about it, and ultimately claim authority over it. That is why he describes Orientalism as a Western style for **dominating, restructuring, and having authority over the Orient**.

The larger point is that **knowledge and power are connected**. If Western scholars, writers, politicians, and artists become the people who supposedly possess authoritative knowledge about “the East,” they also gain the ability to define what the East is. At the same time, the West defines itself through this contrast. Europe becomes rational because the Orient is imagined as irrational; Europe becomes modern because the Orient is called backward; Europe becomes civilized because the Orient is portrayed as primitive or dangerous. Said therefore argues that representations of the East simultaneously construct an image of the East **and strengthen Western identity and power**.

For your class, this becomes a way to analyze Asian American media. Instead of simply asking, “Is this character a bad stereotype?”, Said encourages you to ask **who is defining this person, how they are being constructed as different, what Western norm they are being compared against, and what power relationship that representation supports**.

## Week 2

### Antonio Gramsci — “History of the Subaltern Classes,” “The Concept of Ideology,” and “Cultural Themes: Ideological Material”

Gramsci first discusses the relationship between dominant groups and **subaltern**, or subordinate, groups. Dominant classes tend to be more unified because they have control over the state and political institutions, while subordinate groups are often fragmented. He argues that studying subaltern groups requires looking at how they form, how they interact with dominant political groups, how they organize themselves, and whether they eventually develop independent political consciousness.

From there, Gramsci makes one of his most important distinctions. A dominant social group exercises power in two ways: **domination** and **intellectual and moral leadership**. Domination means direct coercion or force against opponents. Leadership means persuading allied and subordinate groups to accept your worldview and leadership. This second form is what becomes central to the idea of **hegemony**. A ruling group cannot survive through force alone; it also needs some degree of consent.

In “The Concept of Ideology,” Gramsci argues against treating every ideology as simply a stupid or false idea. He distinguishes arbitrary ideas from historically important ideologies. A powerful ideology can **organize huge groups of people and shape the terrain on which they understand themselves, society, and political struggle**. In other words, ideas can become almost like material forces when enough people accept them.

Finally, in “Cultural Themes: Ideological Material,” Gramsci explains that ideology needs actual institutions through which it can circulate. He considers the **press** especially important, but he also includes schools, libraries, churches, organizations, clubs, architecture, and even street layouts and street names. Anything that influences public opinion can become part of the material structure through which ideology is maintained. That is why his theory matters for media: media is not just showing us ideas; it is part of the larger social machinery through which certain ideas become **common sense**.

The easiest way to remember Gramsci is:

**Force makes people obey. Hegemony makes people think the existing order makes sense.**

### Kellner and Durham — “Culture, Ideology, and Hegemony”

Kellner and Durham begin by arguing that media and culture need to be understood as parts of larger social systems. Movies, television, music, newspapers, and other cultural products do not exist separately from capitalism, politics, race, gender, class, or other social relationships. Cultural studies therefore asks how media emerges from particular societies and how it participates in their conflicts.

Their central idea is that **“there are no innocent texts.”** Something can be created primarily as entertainment and still carry assumptions about race, gender, sexuality, class, family, violence, politics, or other social issues. Cultural texts can reproduce dominant relationships, but they can also criticize or resist them. This does not mean every filmmaker is secretly trying to push a political agenda. Rather, cultural works are produced inside societies whose values and assumptions inevitably influence them.

They then explain **ideology** using Marx and Engels. Different historical systems develop different dominant ideas. Feudal society promoted values such as piety, honor, valor, and chivalry. Capitalism made individualism, competition, markets, and profit appear more natural. Contemporary global capitalism promotes globalization, digital technology, and market expansion. Ideology therefore changes historically, but it repeatedly helps make the interests of powerful groups look like ordinary common sense.

Kellner and Durham then connect this to race, gender, and other forms of inequality. Sexist ideology can make male dominance seem natural; racist ideology can make racial hierarchy seem normal. Drawing on Gramsci, they explain that social stability comes from a combination of **domination and hegemony**. Police and military power can enforce an order, while schools, religion, media, and popular culture encourage people to consent to it. The reading therefore teaches you to look at culture and ask: **What values does this make look normal, whose interests do those values serve, and are those values being reinforced or challenged?**

### Stuart Hall — “Encoding/Decoding”

Hall begins by criticizing the traditional **sender → message → receiver** communication model. He thinks this model makes communication look too simple, as though a producer puts a meaning into a message and that exact meaning travels directly into an audience member's head. Hall instead breaks communication into connected but separate moments: **production, circulation, distribution/consumption, and reproduction**. Each stage has its own conditions, meaning one stage cannot completely guarantee what happens at the next.

For something to circulate through television or other media, it has to take a **discursive form**. In simpler language, reality has to be turned into signs, images, words, and stories. A real event cannot simply enter television unchanged. Someone has to choose how to film it, describe it, edit it, and categorize it. This is why Hall argues that representation is not a transparent copy of reality.

Hall spends considerable time explaining **codes**. Some cultural codes are learned so early and used so frequently that they feel natural rather than constructed. A television image can look like reality itself, but it is still a representation organized through codes. That is important ideologically because when a meaning feels “obvious,” people are less likely to notice that cultural assumptions helped produce it.

Hall then discusses **preferred meanings**. Media texts do not have infinitely open meanings; producers and societies structure some interpretations as more legitimate or natural than others. Dominant institutions therefore shape what he calls the preferred reading. Still, audiences are active rather than completely passive.

He identifies three major decoding positions. A **dominant-hegemonic reading** accepts the preferred meaning. A **negotiated reading** accepts the broad dominant framework but modifies it according to personal circumstances. An **oppositional reading** fully understands the preferred message but rejects its framework and interprets it through an alternative one. This means the producer can attempt to encode a particular message without being able to guarantee that audiences will decode it that way.

For the exam, the heart of Hall is:

**Media constructs meaning → producers can prefer one interpretation → audiences can accept, modify, or reject it.**

## Week 3

### Dhingra and Rodriguez — “Responses to Mainstream Media”

This reading shifts attention away from only studying stereotypes produced by mainstream media and asks how **Asian Americans themselves respond**. Asian American communities have created newspapers, activist publications, independent media, art, music, and other forms of self-representation to respond to mainstream portrayals.

However, the authors complicate the idea that self-representation automatically solves the problem. Asian Americans are an extremely diverse group with different ethnicities, generations, immigration histories, political positions, gender identities, sexualities, religions, and class positions. Because of that diversity, **one Asian American media source cannot automatically speak for everyone**.

The reading discusses tensions inside community media. Some ethnic newspapers, for example, may avoid airing internal disagreements or “dirty laundry” because they want to present a united community to outsiders. Publications such as *Gidra*, on the other hand, were more willing to show disagreements and internal political differences. This raises a major question: does solidarity require presenting unity, or does genuine representation require acknowledging conflict?

The discussion of newer and transnational media creates another complication. Media such as K-pop can increase Asian cultural visibility globally, but success in Western markets can still depend partly on meeting **Western cultural aesthetics or Western expectations about Asian culture**. So being visible does not necessarily mean escaping Western power.

The article's overall point is that **representation from within the community is important, but “Asian American” is too complicated to have one completely representative voice**. The difficulty of representing the diversity of Asian American identities is itself part of what we need to analyze.

### Peter Feng — “In Search of Asian American Cinema”

Feng begins with a deceptively simple question: **What is Asian American cinema?** He admits he does not have a permanent answer because even his own definition keeps changing. The phrase assumes there is some coherent thing called “Asian American culture,” but Feng immediately questions whether such a unified culture really exists.

He next stresses the enormous diversity contained within “Asia.” Different countries have different languages, ethnic groups, histories, and political conflicts. Even the word **Asian** changes meaning depending on location. In England, Feng notes that it primarily refers to Pakistani and Indian people, whereas in the United States it usually emphasizes East and Southeast Asian peoples. This shows that racial labels are **socially and historically produced**, not naturally fixed.

Feng then explains that immigrants from different Asian countries did not automatically think of themselves as one community after arriving in the United States. In Hawaii, for example, plantation owners could exploit divisions between Chinese, Japanese, Korean, and Filipino workers. Later generations increasingly recognized that American racism often treated them all as “Orientals” regardless of their differences. From this came a political Asian American coalition. Feng therefore says the ideal Asian American label should express **solidarity while still recognizing diversity**.

This creates a problem when defining cinema. Should Asian American cinema be determined by the filmmaker's ethnicity? By the characters? By the topic? By the intended audience? If you become extremely specific—Chinese American cinema, Japanese American cinema, Korean American cinema—you quickly discover internal differences within each category as well.

Feng also examines the film market. The label **Asian American cinema** already exists because film festivals, funding organizations, critics, and distributors use it. That can be useful because the category provides actual resources and opportunities for filmmakers. At the same time, filmmakers complain that critics often view their work only through ethnicity. A white filmmaker may be analyzed for artistic style, while an Asian American filmmaker may only be asked what the film says about being Asian American.

Feng ultimately resists a rigid answer. He is interested most in **Asian American perspectives**, whether filmmakers are discussing explicitly Asian American subjects or something broader. His point is that the category can create political solidarity and practical opportunities without requiring everyone inside it to tell the same story.

### Benshoff and Griffin — “Asian Americans and American Film”

Benshoff and Griffin place Asian American film representation inside the longer history of **Orientalism**. American cinema inherited ideas that treated “the Orient” as exotic, mysterious, primitive, sensual, dangerous, or fundamentally different from the West. Instead of representing the diversity of Asian cultures, Hollywood often created one generalized **stock Oriental**.

These stock characters appeared in many forms. Asian men could be sinister villains, servants, laundry workers, cooks, or supposedly inscrutable figures. Asian women could become exotic sexual objects or dangerous temptresses. Meanwhile, white actors frequently played the most important Asian characters in **yellowface**, while real Asian actors were restricted to smaller roles.

The reading shows that even apparently “positive” stereotypes remain complicated. **Charlie Chan**, for example, could be intelligent, capable, and heroic, especially compared with openly villainous figures like **Fu Manchu**. But Charlie Chan was still built through racial stereotypes and was frequently performed by white actors. A stereotype does not become harmless simply because the traits assigned to the group are flattering.

The larger argument is that Hollywood historically treated Asian peoples less as complicated individual subjects and more as **racial signs that served Western stories**. That connects directly to Said: American film repeatedly constructed an imagined Oriental against which white Western characters could appear normal, civilized, masculine, or heroic.

### Dhingra and Rodriguez — “Asian Americans as Racial, Ethnic, Gender, and Sexual Minorities”

The reading begins by complicating the tendency to understand American race exclusively through a **black-white binary**. That binary is essential to understanding U.S. racial history, but Asian Americans often occupy a different and contradictory position. They may be treated as economically successful or socially desirable in some contexts while still being seen as permanently foreign.

This leads to the **model minority stereotype**. Asian Americans are represented as hardworking, intelligent, disciplined, family-oriented, and economically successful. Although this appears positive, it can erase Asian Americans who experience poverty or discrimination and make structural inequality harder to recognize. It can also be used politically to compare Asians with other racial minorities and suggest that racism can simply be overcome through hard work.

At the same time, Asian Americans can be represented through **Yellow Peril**, which casts them as foreign threats. Asians may supposedly threaten jobs, economic power, white women, national security, or American culture itself. One of the important things the reading shows is that model minority and Yellow Peril are **not true opposites**. An Asian American can be praised for success while that same success becomes evidence of dangerous competition or “takeover.”

The reading also examines the way race intersects with **gender and sexuality**. Asian men have historically been portrayed as either emasculated and undesirable or as sexual threats to white women. Asian women have been portrayed as submissive sexual objects or dangerous Dragon Ladies. These examples show that racial stereotypes do not operate separately from gender and sexuality.

The major takeaway is that Asian Americans can occupy a contradictory racial position: **successful but foreign, accepted but excluded, praised but potentially threatening**.

### Robert B. Ito — “A Certain Slant: A Brief History of Hollywood Yellowface”

Ito traces yellowface back before Hollywood to older American stage traditions. White performers were acting as Asians for audiences that sometimes had little or no real contact with Asian people. As a result, the theatrical stereotype of “Chineseness” could become familiar to Americans **before actual Chinese people were familiar to them**.

Hollywood continued these traditions partly because of **job protection for white performers**. Asian actors were usually allowed only limited stereotypical roles such as houseboys, cooks, laundry workers, war enemies, or sidekicks. When an Asian character had a large starring role, studios frequently gave it to a white actor instead.

Ito then walks through the excuses Hollywood used. Studios claimed they were simply **“giving audiences what they want.”** They also claimed there were not enough “qualified” Asian actors. Ito points out the circular logic: Asian actors could not get major roles because they supposedly lacked experience, but they could never gain experience because major roles went to white actors. This is the **catch-22** at the center of the article.

Another argument was that white actors actually made better “Orientals” because they knew how to perform the expected stereotype. Ito jokes that this may have been true in a strange sense: white actors were trying to play an “Oriental,” while Asian actors might simply have been trying to play **human beings**. The industry therefore preferred a recognizable stereotype over realistic Asian characters.

Ito later connects this history to controversies such as *Miss Saigon*. Arguments about **artistic freedom** and casting could sound neutral, but he points out that enormous financial interests were involved. Ultimately the article shows that yellowface was not merely a representation problem. It was also a **labor and power problem**: white institutions controlled Asian images while simultaneously excluding Asian performers.

### Krystyn Moon — *Yellowface: Creating the Chinese in American Popular Music and Performance*

Moon expands the study of racial stereotypes from visual representation into **music, theatre, and sound**. During the nineteenth century, Western observers frequently described Chinese music as unpleasant noise rather than legitimate music. Their descriptions often suggested that Chinese people lacked proper musical taste or sophistication.

The important point is that they were evaluating Chinese musical traditions using **Western musical rules as though those rules were universal**. Because Chinese music sounded different from Western music, commentators treated difference as evidence of inferiority rather than trying to understand Chinese music on its own terms. This connects directly with Said's Orientalism: Western observers turn themselves into the authority who gets to define whether another culture is civilized.

Moon also describes theatrical yellowface. White performers used **dialect, makeup, posture, costumes, lyrics, scenery, and other techniques** to manufacture a recognizable Chinese racial character. These elements worked together to mark Chinese bodies as foreign and inferior.

Importantly, the representation was not always simply hateful. Chinese culture could also be portrayed as attractive, fascinating, colorful, or exotic. Moon therefore shows how **fascination and disgust can coexist**. Something can be desired precisely because it has been constructed as foreign and exotic.

Overall, the reading demonstrates that race was produced not just through physical appearance but through **sound, speech, movement, clothing, and performance**.

## Week 4

### Dhingra and Rodriguez — “The Media and Representations of Race”

This reading focuses broadly on the social importance of media. Media does not simply entertain people; it can act as a **teacher of values, beliefs, ideologies, and assumptions**. Because people consume media repeatedly, media can influence what kinds of people, behaviors, occupations, relationships, and social groups appear normal.

Representation matters both through **presence and absence**. When a group repeatedly appears in powerful or prestigious roles, media can confer status on that group. When a group is rarely represented, or represented only through a limited stereotype, audiences receive a different message about its social importance.

The reading also complicates the optimistic idea that the Internet automatically democratizes representation. Digital media allows more people to publish their own content, but access to technology, resources, attention, and influence remains unequal. The **digital divide** therefore means that the ability to put something online does not mean everyone possesses equal media power.

The broader point is that media helps participate in the **construction of racial meaning**, not simply reflect ideas about race that already exist.

### Ono and Pham — Introduction to *Asian Americans and the Media*

Ono and Pham begin from the idea that media representations have to be studied historically. A modern image of an Asian American character does not appear in isolation. Audiences and media producers inherit older images and stereotypes that have accumulated over time. That is why understanding present-day representation requires understanding its **historical development and durability**.

They define media representations as strategies through which Asians and Asian Americans are characterized. Repeated images, stories, and narrative patterns become part of both **individual and social memory**. Because these images are remembered, older representational patterns can return even when the people reproducing them do not consciously intend to repeat a stereotype.

The authors therefore emphasize the idea of **media constructions**. Media representations are created through choices: whom to cast, how someone speaks, what story they appear in, how the camera presents them, what gets included, and what gets left out. Because these are constructions rather than fixed natural truths, they can also be criticized and changed.

Ono and Pham caution against simply labeling every representation either “positive” or “negative.” A seemingly positive image can still reproduce racial hierarchy. Their model-minority example makes this clear: portraying Asian Americans as successful can be used to separate them from Black, Latino, and Native American communities and make inequality appear to be a result of group behavior rather than structural conditions.

They introduce **media racial hegemony**, which they define as media's role in both **continuing and contesting racial and colonial power relations**. Media therefore is not uniformly racist or uniformly liberating. It is a battleground in which older racial power can be reproduced while alternative producers and audiences also challenge it.

Their discussion of globalization makes a similar point. Modern technologies make distant cultures feel close, but that proximity can be misleading. People may see many images of a culture while lacking the context necessary to understand it. Increased exposure can therefore create an **illusion of knowledge** and sometimes make people appear even more exotic or strange.

### Film — *The Mask of Fu Manchu* (1932)

The film begins with Western characters searching for the tomb of **Genghis Khan** and particularly his sword and mask. These artifacts supposedly carry enormous symbolic power. Fu Manchu also wants them because possessing them could allow him to rally Asian peoples together. The plot therefore quickly turns archaeological adventure into a racial conflict between **the West and a threatening Asia**.

Fu Manchu is constructed as the classic **Yellow Peril villain**. He is highly intelligent and technologically sophisticated, but those abilities are portrayed as sinister. His laboratories, torture devices, strange rituals, exotic surroundings, and elaborate schemes make the Orient appear mysterious, cruel, and dangerous. Western intelligence and science appear legitimate, while Fu Manchu's intelligence and science become threatening.

The racial threat is also **sexual**. Fu Manchu's fantasy of Asian victory includes white women, making racial conquest inseparable from fears about interracial sex and reproduction. The white woman therefore becomes a symbol of the racial future of the West. This fits the Yellow Peril idea described in the readings: Asian men do not only threaten territory or jobs; they supposedly threaten **white womanhood and the white family**.

Fu Manchu's daughter, **Fah Lo Suee**, adds a gendered version of Orientalism. She is attractive, powerful, sexually assertive, and cruel. Instead of portraying female independence positively, the film turns it into evidence that she is dangerous. She therefore anticipates the **Dragon Lady** stereotype.

Using Hall, the likely preferred reading is that Fu Manchu and his followers represent a dangerous Oriental threat and that the white Western heroes are justified in stopping them. A 1930s audience already familiar with Yellow Peril imagery would have had cultural codes available for reading the movie that way, although Hall reminds us that no audience interpretation can ever be completely guaranteed.

## Week 5

### Ono and Pham — “The Persistence of Yellow Peril Discourse”

Ono and Pham define **Yellow Peril** as a recurring discourse in which Asians and Asian Americans are represented as threatening to invade, take over, or negatively transform the United States while white Americans are positioned as vulnerable. The stereotype is therefore about more than dislike; it imagines Asians as a danger to the **nation itself**.

They trace this fear historically. Earlier European fears of eastern invasion existed long before modern Asian American stereotypes, including fears surrounding Persian and Mongol power. In the United States, the discourse became particularly important around **Chinese immigration in the nineteenth century**.

Chinese immigrants were portrayed as unassimilable, immoral, dirty, diseased, addicted to opium, sexually dangerous, and economically threatening. Media representations and political debate reinforced one another. Yellow Peril discourse therefore helped create a climate in which restrictions such as the **Chinese Exclusion Act of 1882** appeared reasonable or necessary.

The authors' major historical argument is that Yellow Peril is **flexible**. Its target can move depending on current political conditions. Chinese immigrants could become the threat during one era; Japanese people could become a military threat during WWII and an economic threat decades later; China or Asian Americans could later be tied to espionage, economic competition, crime, or other fears.

That flexibility shows that Yellow Peril does not simply describe actual behavior by Asian groups. The threat is **constructed and repeatedly adapted to American political anxieties**. The people being targeted change, but the underlying story remains: **Asia threatens America**.

### Robert G. Lee — Introduction, “Yellowface,” from *Orientals*

Lee opens with a modern example: a *National Review* cover depicting Bill Clinton, Hillary Clinton, and Al Gore in yellowface during a political fundraising scandal involving Asian donors. Rather than treating political corruption generally, the image specifically racialized **Asian money as polluting American politics**. Lee uses this to demonstrate that old racial imagery can survive and reappear in modern political contexts.

He then explains what yellowface does. Yellowface exaggerates supposedly Asian features—eye shape, skin color, teeth, hair, facial shape—and presents them as natural racial differences. But Lee argues that **race is not a category of nature**. Society chooses certain physical characteristics and gives them racial meaning. Race is therefore an ideology through which inequalities of wealth and power can be made to seem biological or natural.

The **Ozawa case** demonstrates this. Takao Ozawa argued that his skin was physically white and that he was culturally assimilated into American society. The Supreme Court nevertheless ruled that Japanese ancestry prevented him from being legally white. Actual color therefore did not determine racial classification.

The **Thind case** exposed the opposite problem. Bhagat Singh Thind could claim scientific classification as Caucasian, but the Supreme Court still said he was not legally white because ordinary Americans would not recognize an Indian man as white. Taken together, the cases demonstrate that racial categories change their reasoning in order to preserve social boundaries.

Lee describes yellowface's major function as marking the Oriental as **“indelibly alien.”** This leads to an important distinction between being foreign and being alien. Something foreign exists outside the nation. An alien is physically inside the nation but is imagined as fundamentally belonging somewhere else. This explains why Asian Americans can be born in America and still be treated as **perpetual foreigners**.

Popular culture matters because it helps create society's **“common understanding”** of who counts as a real American. Movies, cartoons, newspapers, and other cultural forms help define race by repeatedly marking particular bodies as inside or outside the national community.

Lee then identifies his **Six Faces of the Oriental**: the pollutant, coolie, deviant, Yellow Peril, model minority, and gook. These are historically different stereotypes, but all of them construct the Oriental as an **alien body in relation to the American national family**.

The model minority may look like an exception because it appears complimentary. Yet Lee shows that apparently successful assimilation can still support racial hierarchy. Asian Americans remain a specially marked racial group, and their supposed success can be used against other minorities. The stereotype therefore does not necessarily produce full belonging.

### Film — *Slaying the Dragon*

*Slaying the Dragon* examines the long history of **Asian and Asian American women in American movies and television**. Instead of treating individual stereotypical characters as isolated mistakes, the documentary places them into a repeating historical pattern.

One major pattern is the submissive Asian woman: the **geisha or Lotus Blossom**. She is gentle, obedient, sexually available, loyal, and willing to sacrifice herself, particularly for a Western man. The stereotype makes an unequal gender relationship look romantic or natural.

The opposite stereotype is the **Dragon Lady**. She is confident, sexually assertive, clever, manipulative, and powerful. But instead of that power being celebrated, it becomes the reason audiences are supposed to fear her. Together, the two stereotypes create a double bind: an Asian woman who is submissive can be desirable, while an Asian woman who possesses power becomes dangerous.

The documentary connects these images to **colonialism and militarism**. American and European men often encountered Asian women under unequal political conditions involving war, military occupation, prostitution, domestic service, or colonial rule. Hollywood then transformed those unequal historical relationships into romantic or sexual fantasies.

The documentary also interviews Asian American women about the consequences of these representations. Stereotypes influence expectations in dating, workplaces, sexuality, professional authority, and everyday interaction. Asian American women may have their personalities interpreted through media images before people know them personally.

The film's broader point is that **representation has material consequences**. An image on a screen can shape what real people expect Asian American women to look like, want, or tolerate.

## Week 6

### Sheridan Prasso — “Mystery, Sex, Fear, and Desire: A Brief History”

Prasso begins by arguing that Western understandings of Asia have been built around **mystery, sex, fear, and desire for thousands of years**. Westerners inherit these cultural ideas even when they do not know their historical origins. As a result, what people think they “know” about Asian women may actually come from centuries of repeated Western fantasy rather than direct knowledge.

She uses **Empress Dowager Tzu Hsi** as one of the clearest examples of how this process works. Western accounts portrayed the Chinese ruler as sexually depraved, cruel, murderous, manipulative, and dangerous. Prasso explains that much of this reputation came from fabricated or unreliable sources. Nevertheless, the stories circulated as legitimate scholarship.

Even when those sources were later exposed, the image remained culturally powerful. Tzu Hsi became a model for the **Dragon Lady**: the powerful Asian woman whose authority is explained through sexuality, cruelty, and manipulation. This shows how difficult stereotypes are to erase once they become embedded in supposedly authoritative knowledge.

Prasso then traces Western fantasies further back through travelers such as **Marco Polo**. European accounts depicted Asia as a place of extraordinary luxury, strange customs, and sexual abundance. Stories about sexually available Asian women appealed to European readers and were repeatedly circulated by later writers.

The cultural context of Europe mattered. In Victorian society, respectable women were expected to be extremely **sexually restrained**. When Western men encountered or heard stories about societies with different sexual norms, ordinary differences could seem extremely exotic. Asia became a fantasy space where Western men projected sexual desires that their own culture restricted.

Colonialism intensified this process. Western travelers, missionaries, businessmen, soldiers, and officials did not meet a representative sample of Asian women under equal circumstances. Many encounters occurred within unequal systems where Asian women were servants, courtesans, prostitutes, mistresses, or colonial subjects. Western men then generalized those particular encounters into claims about what **all Asian women were naturally like**.

Prasso also shows how stereotypes can transform when political power changes. During WWII, Japan could be portrayed as a **masculine, aggressive military danger**. After Japan's defeat and occupation, the country's representation became more feminine, submissive, and available to American power. The people had not suddenly changed; the **political relationship had**.

Hollywood later absorbed these much older European Orientalist ideas and circulated them on an enormous scale. The Dragon Lady, geisha, submissive Asian lover, mysterious Oriental woman, and other stereotypes therefore are not simply Hollywood inventions. Hollywood **codified and amplified centuries of Western fantasy**.

Prasso's central argument is that the so-called **Asian Mystique tells us more about Western history, desire, colonial power, and imagination than it tells us about actual Asian women**.

# Big Picture Across the Readings

**Said:** Who has the power to **define the East**?

**Kellner/Durham:** Media texts always contain **values and ideology**.

**Gramsci:** Ideology becomes powerful when society accepts it as **common sense**.

**Hall:** Producers can encode dominant meanings, but **audiences do not have to accept them**.

**Feng:** Even the category **“Asian American” is constructed and politically complicated**.

**Benshoff/Griffin, Ito, Moon:** Hollywood and performance **manufacture the Oriental stereotype** through casting, images, sound, accent, costume, and yellowface.

**Dhingra/Rodriguez:** Asian Americans occupy contradictory racial positions—**model minority and Yellow Peril can exist together**.

**Ono/Pham:** These representations **persist historically**, change form, and help organize racial power.

**Lee:** Race itself gets socially constructed on the body, and Asian Americans are repeatedly marked as **alien rather than fully American**.

**Slaying the Dragon + Prasso:** Orientalism is also **gendered and sexualized**, and stereotypes of Asian women come out of colonialism, militarism, and Western fantasy.
