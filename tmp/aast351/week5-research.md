## Q36. How do Ono and Pham, alongside Lee, explain the persistence of yellow-peril discourse?
Ono and Pham argue that yellow-peril discourse persists by adapting to new political and media contexts: Asians are repeatedly represented as a collective threat, even when the particular threat changes. Lee’s account helps explain the pattern: popular culture’s racial images are historically produced and reworked during social crises. Read together, the authors show that newer portrayals can carry older assumptions about Asian alienness and danger. [Ono and Pham, Chapter 2 scan](https://asame30.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2017/02/ono-and-pham-ch-2.pdf); [Lee, *Orientals*, publisher overview](https://tupress.temple.edu/books/orientals).

## Q37. How does the “perpetual foreigner” stereotype threaten national belonging?
The perpetual-foreigner stereotype treats Asian Americans as outsiders regardless of citizenship, birthplace, or cultural knowledge. It makes racial appearance stand in for foreignness and casts Asian belonging as conditional: a person may be accepted as American only until a crisis revives suspicion about loyalty or national origin. Lee’s six recurring images all position Asians as alien or threatening to the nation-family, while Ono and Pham trace how media representations make such assumptions familiar. [Lee, Temple University Press](https://tupress.temple.edu/books/orientals); [Ono and Pham, Chapter 2](https://asame30.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2017/02/ono-and-pham-ch-2.pdf).

## Q38. What is Lee’s distinction between “alien” and “foreign,” and how does the model-minority image fit?
“Foreign” can describe an actual tie to another country; “alien,” in Lee’s analysis, is a racial position assigned within U.S. culture that marks Asian Americans as not fully belonging even when they are American. The model-minority image seems to offer acceptance by praising Asian success and assimilation, but belonging remains conditional on fitting that approved role. The image also conceals differences and racism. Lee’s publisher describes the six images as portraying the Oriental as alien and as a threat to the national family. [Lee, introduction excerpt](https://www.scribd.com/document/369791653/Orientals-Asian-Americans-in-Popular-Culture-by-Robert-G-Lee1375-Ch1); [Temple University Press](https://tupress.temple.edu/books/orientals).

## Q39. What is Lee’s definition of yellow peril, and what are the six faces?
Yellow peril casts Asians as a collective danger to white America and national security, with the threat shifting between immigration, labor, military power, and geopolitics. Lee identifies six related images: pollutant, coolie, deviant, yellow peril, model minority, and gook. They are not simply six individual stereotypes; they are historically specific ways popular culture marks Asians as alien and threatening. [Lee, *Orientals*, publisher overview](https://tupress.temple.edu/books/orientals); [Lee, searchable intro excerpt](https://www.scribd.com/document/369791653/Orientals-Asian-Americans-in-Popular-Culture-by-Robert-G-Lee1375-Ch1).

## Q40. Why does Ono and Pham’s “persistence” argument matter for politics and representation?
If yellow-peril discourse persists, media images do political work: they can make Asian immigration, economic competition, or international relations appear as evidence of a racial threat. Ono and Pham emphasize that historically, non-Asian institutions produced many dominant images of Asians; repeated representations can therefore shape public understanding while leaving Asian Americans little control over how they appear. Their argument directs attention to who creates media and whose perspective becomes common sense. [Ono and Pham, Chapter 2 scan](https://asame30.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2017/02/ono-and-pham-ch-2.pdf); [Google Books, book contents and overview](https://books.google.com/books/about/Asian_Americans_and_the_Media.html?id=1NQ7PZhiO0sC).

## Q41. How does Lee use “yellowface” to explain racialization?
Lee treats yellowface as a cultural practice that makes “Asian” visible through conventional signs, caricature, and performance. It is not limited to a white actor wearing makeup: it is a broader system that turns diverse Asian people into a legible racial type and repeats that type through popular culture. In this way, representation helps produce the racial category it appears merely to depict. The assigned introduction is available as a third-party searchable scan; Temple’s publisher confirms Lee’s argument about popular culture generalizing racial images across Asian Americans. [Lee, intro excerpt](https://www.scribd.com/document/369791653/Orientals-Asian-Americans-in-Popular-Culture-by-Robert-G-Lee1375-Ch1); [Temple University Press](https://tupress.temple.edu/books/orientals).

## Q42. What is the primary function of yellowface, according to Lee?
Yellowface makes racial difference appear natural and obvious by staging Asian identity as a set of recognizable visual and behavioral cues. That performance reassures a mainstream audience that it can identify, classify, and keep “the Oriental” outside the national norm. Lee’s broader argument is that these popular images serve dominant cultural purposes and help reproduce alienness. This is a paraphrase of Lee’s assigned introduction; the publisher summary supports the wider claim, while the accessible chapter scan is third-party hosted. [Lee, introduction excerpt](https://www.scribd.com/document/369791653/Orientals-Asian-Americans-in-Popular-Culture-by-Robert-G-Lee1375-Ch1); [Temple University Press](https://tupress.temple.edu/books/orientals).

## Q43. How does popular culture shape perceptions of Asian Americans in Lee’s account?
Popular culture does not simply reflect what audiences already think; it supplies recurring images through which Asian Americans are interpreted. Lee argues that images formed in specific historical moments can be generalized across Asian groups and carried forward into later periods. The result is a contradiction: stereotypes change from pollutant or coolie to model minority or gook, yet continue to mark Asians as alien and define the limits of American belonging. [Lee, *Orientals*, Temple University Press](https://tupress.temple.edu/books/orientals); [Lee, introduction excerpt](https://www.scribd.com/document/369791653/Orientals-Asian-Americans-in-Popular-Culture-by-Robert-G-Lee1375-Ch1).

## Q44. What racial definition of “white” underlies *Ozawa* and *Thind*?
In *Ozawa v. United States* (1922), the Court held that Takao Ozawa, a Japanese immigrant, was not eligible for naturalization under the law’s “free white persons” category; it interpreted whiteness through Caucasian classification. In *United States v. Bhagat Singh Thind* (1923), the Court rejected Thind’s claim to whiteness based on anthropological classification and instead used “common understanding.” The cases show how the Court shifted its test while reaching exclusionary outcomes for Asian applicants. [LOC official *Ozawa* opinion](https://www.loc.gov/item/usrep260178/); [LOC official *Thind* opinion](https://www.loc.gov/item/usrep261204/).

## Q45. How does *Slaying the Dragon* connect stereotypes to colonialism?
The film connects portrayals of Asian women to U.S. political relationships with Asia: the images on screen shift alongside international relations, while preserving American power to define Asian women as exotic, submissive, or dangerous. This is a colonial pattern because Asia becomes an object of U.S. fantasy and control, and Asian women’s identities are organized around that outside gaze. Columbia’s filmography describes the documentary’s explicit focus on how international relations shaped film portrayals. [Columbia University filmography entry](https://www.columbia.edu/cu/weai/exeas/films/slaying-dragon.html); [Women Make Movies study guide](https://www.wmm.com/uploads/2019/02/dragon_study.pdf).

## Q46. How do these stereotypes affect real Asian and Asian American women?
Stereotypes shape how women are perceived before they speak or act: the submissive/exotic figure can invite fetishization and assumptions of availability, while the “dragon lady” casts assertiveness as deceitful or threatening. The film’s interviews ask how screen images affect women’s everyday experiences and professional roles. Columbia’s synopsis identifies those recurring stereotypes and describes the film’s interviews with Asian women. I could verify the film’s scope and study guide, but not inspect its full footage directly. [Columbia University filmography](https://www.columbia.edu/cu/weai/exeas/films/slaying-dragon.html); [Women Make Movies study guide](https://www.wmm.com/uploads/2019/02/dragon_study.pdf).

## Q47. How do Orientalism, militarism, and gendered racial domination relate?
Orientalism constructs “Asia” as exotic, backward, or threatening from a Western point of view; militarism gives those representations political force by positioning Asian people and places within U.S. wars and security narratives. Gendered stereotypes then cast Asian women as sexually available or dangerous, turning domination into a story about feminine character rather than unequal power. *Slaying the Dragon* links changing film images of Asian women to changing international relations, supporting this connection between media fantasy and geopolitical power. [Columbia University filmography](https://www.columbia.edu/cu/weai/exeas/films/slaying-dragon.html); [Ono and Pham, Chapter 2 scan](https://asame30.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2017/02/ono-and-pham-ch-2.pdf).
